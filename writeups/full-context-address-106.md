# David Cameron European address: remember the achieved purpose, name the unresolved problems, offer a reform frame, and make the choice conditional

## Overall structure

```text
past war -> achieved peace -> new prosperity problem
-> British identity and purpose -> three challenges
-> five reform principles -> British consent problem
-> delayed, defined referendum -> in/out tradeoffs
-> reform hope and conditional commitment
```

The address avoids beginning with the referendum itself. It first gives the
European project an achieved purpose—peace—then says its main task has changed
to prosperity. Britain is presented as both independent and open, allowing the
speaker to reject isolation while asking for reform. The long middle section
turns dissatisfaction into five principles, and the referendum is introduced
only after the proposed settlement is described. The final choice is framed as
conditional, informed and costly rather than simple escape.

## Episode 1: use a long historical arc to establish what has already worked

Captions `:00002`–`:00040` move from the destruction of European war to sustained
peace, friendship and the Elysée Treaty, then to the fall of the Berlin Wall.
The European Union’s first purpose—securing peace—is declared achieved, with
tribute to the EU and NATO.

**Communication job:** give reform a reason that does not deny the value of the
existing institution.

**Practice hypothesis:** name the original problem, show the mechanism that
helped solve it, acknowledge the achievement and then explain why the next
problem is different.

**Boundary:** historical success can be selectively narrated. Do not use an
achievement story to end debate about present costs or omissions.

## Episode 2: change the problem while preserving the purpose

Captions `:00041`–`:00061` say the main purpose has shifted from peace to
prosperity and that the new competition comes from outside Europe. Entrepreneurs,
workers and families are named before the speaker promises urgency and
frankness.

**Communication job:** update an institution’s mission without saying its past
purpose was mistaken.

**Practice hypothesis:** distinguish the enduring value from the changing task,
then show who experiences the new task.

## Episode 3: define a national identity as a tension, not a stereotype

Captions `:00062`–`:00169` describe Britain as independent, practical,
sovereignty-minded and open to the world. The European Union is called a means
to prosperity, stability, freedom and democracy, not an end in itself. Britain’s
historical contribution to Europe is then used to reject both isolation and
uncritical integration.

**Communication job:** make a controversial position compatible with multiple
values held by the same audience.

**Practice hypothesis:** name the identity’s tension—independence and openness,
for example—then show how the proposed position serves both sides.

**Caution:** national character language can exclude citizens who do not fit the
story. Historical contribution is not permanent entitlement.

## Episode 4: make difficult questions legitimate before answering them

Captions `:00170`–`:00245` ask why raise fundamental questions during crisis and
answer with three challenges: eurozone problems, competitiveness and a gap in
democratic consent. The speech warns that refusing difficult questions could
push Britain toward exit, while stating that the speaker wants a successful EU.

**Communication job:** justify a disruptive conversation as protection of the
institution rather than hostility to it.

**Practice hypothesis:** state the uncomfortable question, give the risks of
avoiding it, and make the desired outcome explicit.

## Episode 5: diagnose before prescribing

Captions `:00246`–`:00265` describe eurozone change, economic weakness and public
frustration, then argue that “more of the same” will not solve the problems. The
speech announces a new European Union fit for the twenty-first century.

**Communication job:** create a bridge from complaint to a reform proposal.

**Practice hypothesis:** group scattered complaints into a small diagnosis,
state what the default response cannot do, and introduce the replacement frame.

**Limit:** a clean diagnosis may combine different causes. Check whether the
proposed answer actually addresses each one.

## Episode 6: use principles as a map for a complex reform

Captions `:00266`–`:00509` introduce five principles: competitiveness,
flexibility, power returning to member states, democratic accountability and
fairness. The first sections use examples—single-market gaps, regulation,
different national preferences and institutional powers—to turn “reform” into
separate questions.

**Communication job:** keep a long policy argument navigable and let listeners
test each part separately.

**Practice hypothesis:** announce a small set of principles, define each in
plain language, give an example and state the tradeoff or safeguard.

**Caution:** a numbered structure can create a feeling of completeness even when
some principles receive less detail or conflict with others.

## Episode 7: make flexibility mean different rules with shared enforcement

Captions `:00313`–`:00405` argue that Europe contains different sizes, regions
and desired levels of integration. Common rules for the single market are
retained, while a one-size-fits-all approach is rejected. Flexible cooperation
is described as stronger than compulsion.

**Communication job:** defend difference without abandoning a common system.

**Practice hypothesis:** state what must remain common, what may vary, and who
enforces the shared floor.

**Boundary:** flexibility can become unequal treatment or a race downward. The
speech’s examples do not settle those risks.

## Episode 8: turn sovereignty into a power-allocation question

Captions `:00406`–`:00462` argue for powers to flow back to member states and
describe a review of what the EU should do or stop doing. Cooperation is linked
to trade, energy, climate, poverty, security and new members.

**Communication job:** make “less centralization” more concrete than a slogan.

**Practice hypothesis:** list the decisions that should move, the decisions that
should remain shared, and the reason for each placement.

## Episode 9: locate democratic accountability in answerable institutions

Captions `:00463`–`:00509` place accountability in national parliaments and use
named leaders and decisions as examples. Fairness then asks that eurozone
arrangements protect countries inside and outside the currency union.

**Communication job:** connect a constitutional principle to a person or body
that can be questioned.

**Practice hypothesis:** name the decision-maker, the public forum and the
affected interest; do not stop at “accountability.”

## Episode 10: treat consent as an existing problem, not a future threat

Captions `:00510`–`:00573` list British frustration—regulation, legal judgments,
integration, promised referendums and the eurozone. The speaker argues that the
question mark already exists and that ignoring it could make exit more likely.

**Communication job:** make consultation appear responsible rather than merely
reactive.

**Caution:** the list describes public sentiment through the speaker’s framing;
it is not a survey and may not represent all citizens.

## Episode 11: refuse a false binary before offering the real choice

Captions `:00574`–`:00631` reject an immediate vote between the status quo and
leaving. The future EU is said to be changing, so the public should first know
what it is choosing. The proposed choice becomes leaving or joining a reformed
settlement with safeguards, flexibility and national accountability.

**Communication job:** delay a high-stakes decision until its options are more
defined.

**Practice hypothesis:** identify why the current binary is incomplete, describe
the missing settlement, and state when the decision becomes meaningful.

**Limit:** delaying a choice also changes political leverage and may serve the
speaker’s strategy. “Real choice” is a claim that needs scrutiny.

## Episode 12: argue for membership through tradeoffs, not fear alone

Captions `:00632`–`:00825` describe negotiation, the planned referendum, the
appeal of going alone, market access, influence, NATO, legal ties and the Norway
and Switzerland comparisons. The speech says Britain could leave but asks
whether that is the best national interest and warns that exit is one-way.

**Communication job:** make a preferred choice credible by admitting its costs
and the strengths of the alternative.

**Practice hypothesis:** state what each option gains, loses and leaves
unchanged; distinguish legal, economic, security and identity consequences.

**Caution:** comparisons between countries can hide different resources,
institutions and bargaining positions. Historical and economic claims require
checking.

## Episode 13: close with conditional commitment and continued effort

Captions `:00826`–`:00862` reject defeatism, repeat flexible, adaptable and open,
and promise a campaign for the reformed arrangement if negotiation succeeds.
The closing joins national interest, European success and future generations,
then thanks the audience.

**Communication job:** finish with conviction while leaving the commitment
dependent on a stated condition.

**Practice hypothesis:** name the desired outcome, the condition that activates
your commitment and the work you will continue until then.

## Next comparison and product test

Compare long political addresses that honor an institution’s past purpose,
diagnose several failures, use numbered principles, correct a false binary and
close with conditional commitment. Seek cases where history becomes nostalgia,
identity excludes, principles conflict, consultation is performative, or
tradeoff language hides unequal costs.

Prototype a learner card called **Make a hard choice more honest**:

1. State what the current system has achieved.
2. Name the new problem and the people who feel it.
3. Offer two or three principles, each with an owner, example and tradeoff.
4. Describe what each choice gains, loses and leaves uncertain.
5. State the condition for your recommendation and the evidence that would make
   you revise it.
