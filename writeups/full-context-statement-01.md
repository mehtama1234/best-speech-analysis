## Distinguish the things being denied

At 0–11, the speaker rejects claims and attributes objectionable motives to their
sources. Those characterizations remain her assertions; the review does not adopt
them as findings about other people.

At 12–28, the statement denies friendship or a relationship while acknowledging
shared events and an email exchange, which it characterizes as casual. The
rhetorical move is classification: acknowledged contact is argued not to establish
the relationship alleged. Summarizing this as a denial of all contact would be
inaccurate. Equally, acknowledged contact alone does not establish misconduct.

At 29–43, the speaker supplies an alternative chronology and references her own
book. That is a source named within the argument, not independently inspected

## The denials have different scopes

At 44–71, claims concerning media authenticity are followed by categorical denials

These are not interchangeable. A claim about one category cannot automatically
prove a claim about another, and the review has not inspected the underlying
materials required to test any of them.

## The requested process preserves choice but overstates finality

At 99–111, the requested process specifies a public hearing centered on survivors,
sworn testimony and entry into a permanent record. Public storytelling at 106–108

At 112–114, the statement treats the proposed process as the exclusive route to
truth. The transcript does not establish either exclusivity or sufficiency. A
requested hearing is not a hearing that happened; sworn testimony is not by itself
proof that an inquiry is complete or every factual issue resolved. This distinction
is analytical, not procedural or legal advice.
