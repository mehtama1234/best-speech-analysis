## Why the boundary matters

A caption is not necessarily a complete speech act. The Hopper question ends
with a short fragment in the target caption but begins two captions earlier.
Rejecting it solely because the fragment lacks an interrogative opening would
be wrong. Conversely, a nearby question about energy does not turn the later
solar-energy assessment into a question. Context establishes membership in a
question; it does not transfer the label to every adjacent sentence.

The repetition challenge also corrects an overbroad legacy interpretation.
Its surrounding language concerns telling the same story repeatedly, not a
generic invitation to audience reflection. Text supports that distinction;
it does not prove frustration, sarcasm or any other internal state.

Finally, an answer appearing immediately after a question in a transcript is
not proof that the same speaker answered it. Turn attribution and audience
participation require evidence beyond unlabelled caption order. The cricket
interview has explicit transcript turn labels; the falling-down example does
not. These are different evidence strengths.

## What a learner can take from this

Use these as contrasting examples to inspect, not as a validated instruction
to ask more questions. An interview prompt solicits an account; narrated
self-questions present a reflective sequence; a challenge contests an ongoing
exchange; a question-answer pair can organize an explanation. Before trying a
question in a presentation, specify which job it should do and whether the
audience is expected to answer. This is a proposed exercise, not measured
evidence of improved comprehension or persuasion.
