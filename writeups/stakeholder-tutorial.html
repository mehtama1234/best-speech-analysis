Speech Learning System: stakeholder tutorial

A practical guide to learning reusable communication structures from a 395-video speech corpus—while keeping transcript evidence, delivery inspection, hypotheses and validation clearly separate.

What this is

This project helps a learner study how a speaker handles a communication job—opening, explaining, disagreeing, answering pressure, requesting action or closing—and adapt the underlying structure to their own situation.

Copy the decision structure, not the speaker’s identity. Do not imitate status, accent, biography, certainty or body language as if those were techniques.

The learner workflow

  1. Choose the listener problem. Start with the situation, not the celebrity.
  2. Read the surrounding evidence. Identify what the speaker is doing in context.
  3. Name the structure. Write the move as a sequence.
  4. Adapt the language. Rewrite it for your facts, relationship and certainty.
  5. Make one delivery choice. Try a pause, emphasis, pace or turn-taking choice; treat visual/audio data as inspection evidence.
  6. Practice and ask. Get a consenting listener’s restatement and concerns.
  7. Revise or discard. Keep only what improves clarity without distortion or harm.

Verbal, vocal and nonverbal practice

Treat the three layers as separate decisions. The transcript can show words and sequence; audio/video can provide something to inspect; neither layer alone proves intent or effectiveness.

LayerTry thisAsk the listenerAccessible equivalent
VerbalMark the job explicitly: answer, condition, example, repair, limit or next step.What was the point, and what remains uncertain?Put the same signposts in writing, captions or a shared document.
VocalChange one variable: pause before a distinction, shorten a dense clause, stress a contrast word or slow a new term.Did the boundary or priority become easier to hear?Use punctuation, headings, timing controls or a second written pass.
NonverbalTest orientation, turn yield, visible pointing or posture only when available and welcome.Could you follow the move without seeing my body? Did anything create pressure?Make the meaning explicit in words; never require eye contact, gesture or facial expression.

Keep the wording constant while changing one delivery choice. Compare the listener’s restatement, unresolved point and access burden—not your own feeling of confidence. Caption gaps, pitch, volume, face counts and pose geometry are inspection proxies, not evidence of emotion, sincerity, authority, confidence or persuasion.

A five-minute delivery experiment

  1. Choose one job and one retained-caption example.
  2. Write the listener problem, smallest useful outcome, condition and limit.
  3. Make two versions, changing only one vocal or visible choice.
  4. Present them in randomized order to a consenting listener, or review them later without knowing which is which.
  5. Ask for the takeaway, unresolved point, pressure or burden, and preferred access mode.
  6. Log the wording, delivery change, access mode, listener report, non-fit and revision. This is practice evidence, not a general effectiveness result.

Six useful starting patterns

Orient before detail

Situation → stake → route

Tell people where they are, why it matters and where you are going.

Answer, then frame

Direct answer → useful context → condition or limit

Give the requested answer before adding context and exceptions.

Make the abstract inspectable

Claim → bounded example → interpretation → boundary

Use examples to illuminate, not to claim more than they establish.

Make change visible fairly

Earlier view → what changed → present view → genuine overlap

Make revision understandable without caricaturing the earlier position.

Turn credit into responsibility

Contribution/problem → accountable actor → bounded action → support and consent

Name what remains owed, who can act and what support is required.

Close with a retrievable point

Takeaway → next step or uncertainty → release the turn

End with something the listener can repeat or an honest unknown.

One complete practice lesson

Situation: A colleague asks why a project changed direction.

Listener problem: They need to understand what changed without hearing the earlier plan mocked or the new plan presented as certain.

Structure: Earlier view → evidence that changed the view → present plan → genuine overlap → open condition.

Adapted wording: “We originally planned to launch the full version in June. The pilot showed that onboarding was taking twice as long as expected, so I think the responsible move is to release the smaller version first. We still agree on the customer outcome; what depends on the next two weeks is whether we have enough support capacity for the larger launch.”

Delivery experiment: Record one version with a short pause before “so I think” and another with no pause. Keep the words the same. If the setting is visual, test whether a written timeline or shared screen clarifies the change; do not require eye contact or gesture.

Listener check: Ask, “What changed, what do you believe the current plan is, and what is still uncertain?” Also ask whether the wording assigned blame or pressure.

Revision rule: Keep the form only if it improves the listener’s ability to restate the change and uncertainty. If the listener hears “the pilot failed” when that was not established, replace the causal wording with the observed fact. If the overlap is not genuine, remove it rather than using rapport as a disguise.

This is a constructed practice example informed by the corpus patterns; it is not a transcript claim or an outcome result.

Evidence levels and boundaries

LevelSupportsDoes not support
Transcript evidenceWords and local sequence appear in retained captions.Intent, truth, effectiveness or generality.
Recurrence candidateSimilar forms or structures recur.A causal best practice.
Delivery proxyA timestamped audio/video region is available for inspection.Emotion, confidence, honesty or persuasion.
Mechanism hypothesisA reason the form might help is proposed.That listeners experienced the proposed benefit.
ValidationA defined practice produced an observed response in a defined setting.Universal transfer.

Claims about health, history, politics, child welfare, organizations or personal experience require independent checking. Work involving vulnerable people requires consent, dignity, privacy and local leadership.

What stakeholders can review

Review whether the transcript description is accurate, whether each learning move is appropriate for the intended audience, whether the prompts are safe and accessible, whether examples are represented fairly, and whether every claim is traceable to evidence. Keep separate the questions of accuracy, usefulness and measured learner/listener outcome.

The current materials provide transcript-derived learning hypotheses and support structured validation. Independent interpretation, full delivery review, counterexample review, learner validation and listener-outcome testing remain separate gates.
