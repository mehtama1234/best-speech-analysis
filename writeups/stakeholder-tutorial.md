# Speech Learning System: stakeholder tutorial

## What this is

This project turns a large collection of speeches into a practical learning
system. It helps a learner study how a speaker handles a communication job—such
as opening, explaining, disagreeing, answering pressure, asking a question,
requesting action or closing—and then adapt the underlying structure to their
own situation.

The central rule is simple: copy the decision structure, not the speaker's
identity. A useful lesson may include exact wording, sequence, vocal choices,
visible delivery candidates and safety limits, but it is not an instruction to
imitate a celebrity's accent, status, biography, certainty or body language.

## What has been covered

The retained-caption corpus currently contains:

- 395 videos reviewed in full transcript context;
- 102,816 inspected caption segments;
- 4,016 transcript-derived function cards;
- 2,800 structural or sequence cards;
- 18 provisional communication jobs;
- 50 exact phrase-form hypotheses; and
- one starting learning track for every video.

The evidence is stored with source video identifiers, caption text and timing
so that a learner or reviewer can return to the surrounding context. The
numbers describe coverage and retrieval. They do not establish that a form is
universally effective or that a speaker intended the inferred function.

## The learner workflow

1. **Choose the listener problem.** Start with “I need to explain a change” or
   “I need to disagree without losing the relationship,” not with a famous
   speaker or an attractive phrase.
2. **Read the surrounding evidence.** Inspect the full local caption context,
   then identify what the speaker is doing in that moment.
3. **Name the structure.** Write the move as a sequence: for example,
   `earlier view → what changed → present view → genuine overlap`.
4. **Adapt the language.** Rewrite the wording for your relationship, facts,
   certainty and authority. Exact phrases are retrieval handles, not magic
   incantations.
5. **Make one delivery choice.** Try a deliberate pause, emphasis, pace,
   turn-taking choice or visible orientation. Treat visual and audio records as
   inspection evidence, not as proof of confidence, sincerity or persuasion.
6. **Run a low-stakes practice.** Ask a consenting listener what they heard,
   what remains uncertain and whether the delivery created pressure or burden.
7. **Revise or discard.** Keep the structure only when it improves clarity or
   coordination without distorting the issue or harming the relationship.

## Verbal, vocal and nonverbal practice

Treat the three layers as separate decisions. The transcript can show words and
sequence; audio/video can provide something to inspect; neither layer alone
proves intent or effectiveness.

| Layer | Try this | Ask the listener | Accessible equivalent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Verbal | Mark the job explicitly: answer, condition, example, repair, limit or next step. | What was the point, and what remains uncertain? | Put the same signposts in writing, captions or a shared document. |
| Vocal | Change one variable: pause before a distinction, shorten a dense clause, stress a contrast word or slow a new term. | Did the boundary or priority become easier to hear? | Use punctuation, headings, timing controls or a second written pass. |
| Nonverbal | Test orientation, turn yield, visible pointing or posture only when it is available and welcome. | Could you follow the move without seeing my body? Did anything create pressure? | Make the meaning explicit in words; never require eye contact, gesture or facial expression. |

For each experiment, keep the wording constant while changing one delivery
choice. Compare the listener's restatement, unresolved point and access burden;
do not compare your own feeling of confidence. A caption gap, pitch value,
volume value, face count or pose geometry is a navigation or inspection proxy,
not evidence of emotion, sincerity, authority, confidence or persuasion.

### A five-minute delivery experiment

1. Choose one communication job and one retained-caption example.
2. Write the listener problem, smallest useful outcome, condition and limit.
3. Make two versions, changing only one vocal or visible choice.
4. Present them in randomized order to a consenting listener, or review them
   later without knowing which version is which.
5. Ask for the takeaway, unresolved point, pressure or burden, and preferred
   access mode. Keep the result local to this listener and situation.
6. Log the wording, delivery change, media/access mode, listener report,
   non-fit and revision. This is practice evidence, not a general effectiveness
   result.

## Six useful starting patterns

These are current cross-job learning hypotheses. They are not certified best
practices.

### 1. Orient before detail

`Situation → stake → route`

Tell people where they are, why the point matters and where you are going
before asking them to process detail. Use it for openings, briefings and dense
explanations.

### 2. Answer, then frame

`Direct answer → useful context → condition or limit`

Give the person the answer they asked for before adding context. This preserves
orientation while making uncertainty and exceptions visible.

### 3. Make the abstract inspectable

`Claim → bounded example → interpretation → boundary`

An example should illuminate the claim, not pretend to prove more than it can.
Name what the example shows and what it does not show.

### 4. Make change visible fairly

`Earlier view → what changed → present view → genuine overlap`

This makes revision understandable without caricaturing the earlier position.
It is useful for disagreement, learning, apology and policy change.

### 5. Turn credit into responsibility

`Contribution or problem → accountable actor → bounded action → support and consent`

Acknowledgment becomes more useful when it names what remains owed, who can act
and what support or permission is required.

### 6. Close with a retrievable point

`Takeaway → next step or uncertainty → release the turn`

End with something the listener can repeat, a realistic next step or an honest
unknown. Do not use a polished close to conceal missing evidence.

## Exact forms to study

These are small retrieval examples from the phrase inventory. The form is not
the lesson by itself; the surrounding job, wording and limit are the lesson.

| Form | Corpus example | Why it may help | Transfer limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| `in other words` | “old enough, in other words your parents” ([source](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2mqQMA7CSLQ&t=305s)) | Signals a reformulation so the listener can check the meaning. | The rewrite must actually clarify; it cannot repair a false claim. |
| `for example` | “for example, when you were 15” ([source](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2wf1qE7AC8Y&t=21s)) | Marks a case as an illustration rather than leaving its role implicit. | One case is not prevalence, proof or causation. |
| `I think` | “I think that is an extraordinarily…” ([source](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3AIoHLr8nTI&t=91s)) | Can mark a judgment as the speaker’s view and calibrate ownership. | It does not make weak evidence strong or automatically communicate genuine uncertainty. |
| `it depends` | “it depends on what the definition…” ([source](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1QvLB4yEHsI&t=280s)) | Opens a condition that should be named rather than hidden. | Do not use it as a vague escape; state what it depends on. |
| `the point is` | “The point is your generation…” ([source](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-54zUwySKCg&t=989s)) | Helps retrieve the takeaway after examples or argument. | A takeaway still needs a reason, scope and uncertainty. |
| `you can` | “Do what you can wherever you are” ([source](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-54zUwySKCg&t=639s)) | May make a next role visible at a manageable scale. | A request must still name feasibility, resources, consent and what the action cannot solve. |

When adapting one, replace the source claim with your own facts. Keep the
function, not the celebrity’s wording, authority or emotional force. The full
[phrase transfer guide](phrase-transfer-guide.md) contains the 50-form
inventory and its context warnings.

## One complete practice lesson

**Situation:** A colleague asks why a project changed direction.

**Listener problem:** They need to understand what changed without hearing the
earlier plan mocked or the new plan presented as certain.

**Structure:** `Earlier view → evidence that changed the view → present plan →
genuine overlap → open condition`.

**Adapted wording:** “We originally planned to launch the full version in
June. The pilot showed that onboarding was taking twice as long as expected, so
I think the responsible move is to release the smaller version first. We still
agree on the customer outcome; what depends on the next two weeks is whether we
have enough support capacity for the larger launch.”

**Delivery experiment:** Record one version with a short pause before “so I
think” and another with no pause. Keep the words the same. If the setting is
visual, test whether a written timeline or a shared screen clarifies the change;
do not require eye contact or gesture.

**Listener check:** Ask, “What changed, what do I believe the current plan is,
and what is still uncertain?” Also ask whether the wording assigned blame or
pressure.

**Revision rule:** Keep the form only if it improves the listener’s ability to
restate the change and the uncertainty. If the listener hears “the pilot failed”
when that was not established, replace the causal wording with the observed
fact. If the overlap is not genuine, remove it rather than using rapport as a
disguise.

This is a constructed practice example informed by the corpus patterns; it is
not a transcript claim or an outcome result.

## What to record during practice

For each attempt, record the situation, intended listener, structure used,
adapted wording, one vocal or timing choice, one visible-delivery observation,
listener restatement, unresolved point, safety/accessibility concern and next
revision. A listener's report is an outcome observation, not a verdict on the
speaker's character.

## Evidence levels and boundaries

The project deliberately separates several claims:

| Level | What it supports | What it does not support |
|---|---|---|
| Transcript evidence | The words and local sequence appear in the retained captions. | Intent, truth, effectiveness or generality. |
| Recurrence candidate | Similar forms or structures recur in the corpus. | A causal best practice. |
| Delivery proxy | A timestamped audio/video region is available for inspection. | Emotion, confidence, honesty or persuasion. |
| Mechanism hypothesis | A reason the form might help is proposed. | That listeners actually experienced that benefit. |
| Learner/listener validation | A tested practice produced an observed response in a defined setting. | Universal transfer to every audience or culture. |

Claims about health, history, politics, child welfare, organizations or personal
experience require independent checking. Stories involving trauma, children,
privacy, disability or vulnerable communities require consent, dignity and
local leadership; they should never be mined merely because they are powerful
examples.

## What external stakeholders can review

Stakeholders can review whether the system is understandable, whether examples
are represented fairly, whether the practice prompts are safe and accessible,
whether evidence links are traceable, and whether a proposed lesson overclaims.
They should distinguish three questions:

1. Is the transcript description accurate?
2. Is the proposed learning move useful and appropriate for this audience?
3. Has the move improved a defined learner or listener outcome?

The current materials answer the first question provisionally and support
structured work on the second. Independent interpretation, full delivery
review, counterexample review, learner validation and listener-outcome testing
remain separate gates rather than being silently folded into the corpus counts.
