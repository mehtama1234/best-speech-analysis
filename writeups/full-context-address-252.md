## Pattern cards

### A. Lower your status before giving advice

**Mechanic:** begin with thanks, then make yourself the subject of a harmless joke
before asking the audience to listen seriously.

**In this speech:** the parents’ imagined dislike, the ridiculous film achievements
and the fake doctorate all reduce distance between celebrity and graduate.

**Why it works:** the speaker gives up some authority voluntarily, so later advice
feels less like a command from above.

**Guardrail:** self-deprecation should not ask the audience to comfort you, hide harm
or make a less powerful person the joke.

### B. Expose the gap between a title and the skill it does not grant

**Mechanic:** take a real honor, exaggerate what it supposedly authorizes, then let
the absurdity reveal the difference between recognition and competence.

**In this speech:** an honorary doctorate is treated as permission to perform surgery.

**Reusable form:** “This title means something important. It does not mean I can do
the work that requires another kind of training.”

**Use:** helpful when status, credentials or praise may distort expectations.

### C. Put the speaker and audience inside the same imperfection

**Mechanic:** praise the audience, invent an exception, reveal that the exception is
larger and more ordinary than expected, then include yourself.

**In this speech:** the “four students” joke expands to many people and becomes a
shared refusal of perfect graduation stories.

**Why it works:** it creates relief without denying achievement.

### D. Use an absurd future to tell the true past

**Mechanic:** imagine a future version of the listener or speaker, fill it with funny
predictions, then land on the plain fact that matters.

**In this speech:** the future visitor predicts fame and pop-culture references;
the real turn is that the speaker graduated but did not move directly into success.

**Reusable form:** “If I had known the final label, I still would not have known the
messy middle that got me there.”

### E. Call a place a testing lab

**Mechanic:** rename an ordinary environment as a place to try identities and
behaviors before the stakes become high.

**In this speech:** the campus becomes a theater where jokes, characters and
interruptions are tested in front of friends and teachers.

**Why it works:** practice is made visible and permission is attached to a place.

**Guardrail:** experimentation still needs consent, respect for classrooms and a
clear way for others to opt out.

### F. Show encouragement changing the person’s self-permission

**Mechanic:** describe an action that could have produced rejection; instead, a
trusted person invites a repeat, and the invitation changes what the speaker allows
himself to become.

**In this speech:** the professor asks for another interruption, helping the speaker
see himself as funny to people who did not already know him.

**Product question:** what small invitation could give someone permission to try a
new identity without making a promise about the outcome?

### G. Treat failed attempts as darts, not a verdict

**Mechanic:** show many imperfect attempts, keep the target visible, and separate
continued testing from guaranteed success.

**In this speech:** bad stand-up, odd material and repeated performances are darts
thrown at a board until something sticks.

**Why it works:** the metaphor makes iteration concrete and removes the demand that
the first attempt reveal the final talent.

**Guardrail:** “keep trying” must include feedback, adjustment, money, health and
rest; persistence alone cannot overcome every barrier.

### H. Compare fear of failure with fear of never trying

**Mechanic:** name both fears, then ask which future regret should guide the next
choice.

**In this speech:** fear remains present, but the fear of never trying is made larger.

**Use:** powerful for a close, provided the audience is not shamed for choosing
safety or for having constraints.

### I. Set a boundary around critics without pretending criticism is useless

**Mechanic:** accept that some people will dislike the work, preserve the job’s
purpose, and refuse to let every opinion become an identity.

**In this speech:** criticism is kept visible, political satire is defended, and a
body insult is answered with self-directed humor.

**Better practice:** separate criticism of the work, criticism of the person and
abuse; learn from the first, assess the second and set boundaries around the third.

### J. Redefine success with a wider ledger

**Mechanic:** acknowledge public success, then name relationships, service and
contribution as the measures that matter most.

**In this speech:** family and scholarship support join entertainment success in the
definition of a good life.

**Why it works:** it gives graduates more than one scoreboard.

**Guardrail:** do not imply that family, marriage, children or charity are required
for a meaningful life. Let each listener choose their measures.

### K. Give permission to search without turning search into drift

**Mechanic:** normalize uncertainty, name a process of trying, and pair it with a
simple next action and support network.

**In this speech:** graduates are told to enjoy the search, trust their gut and keep
throwing darts, while remembering that they are not alone.

**Reusable form:** “You do not need the whole plan. Choose the next honest test,
notice what you learn and decide what to try next.”

## Product implications

1. **Status-lowering opener coach:** help a speaker find a safe self-joke that reduces
   distance without undermining the message or asking for reassurance.
2. **Title-to-skill checker:** separate what a credential recognizes, what it permits,
   what it does not teach and which expert should be consulted.
3. **Shared-imperfection builder:** create a light audience exercise that celebrates
   achievement while making room for unfinished paths.
4. **Future-to-past story frame:** generate an absurd imagined future, then bring the
   story back to the real uncertain middle and the next step.
5. **Testing-lab planner:** turn a safe environment into experiments with purpose,
   audience consent, feedback, revision and a stopping rule.
6. **Permission moment finder:** identify a teacher, peer or manager’s small action
   that made a new behavior feel possible, and show how to offer that invitation.
7. **Dartboard practice tracker:** log attempts, target, feedback, change, cost,
   recovery and what the speaker learned—not only whether it succeeded.
8. **Fear-versus-regret reflection:** compare the risks of trying and not trying while
   recording real constraints, safety needs and alternative small tests.
9. **Criticism boundary tool:** sort feedback into useful, uncertain, personal and
   abusive; choose response, learning and boundary for each.
10. **Multi-scoreboard success map:** let users define success across craft,
    relationships, health, service, money, freedom and contribution without a fixed
    life template.
11. **Search-to-next-test planner:** convert uncertainty into one small experiment,
    support person, review date and next decision.
