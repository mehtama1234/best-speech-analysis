## Promise is introduced with an explicit capability limit

The chair's opening (10–43) contrasts familiar novelty uses with potential benefits
and harms, singling out employment as a concern. The witness's introduction
(44–81) presents institutional purpose and a printing-press analogy. After naming
possible discoveries and large societal benefits, he explicitly says the then-current
systems cannot yet accomplish those ambitions (76–77). A summary retaining only
the ambitious examples would turn a future aspiration into a present capability
claim that this text itself disclaims.

Use cases and a safety-process account follow (82–116). They supply examples and
asserted organizational procedures, not independent evidence that the procedures
worked or that benefits outweighed risks. The move to government intervention
(117–136) includes a capability threshold rather than an unqualified statement
about every model. Company responsibility is then retained regardless of what
Congress does (137–140). The two responsibilities are presented as complementary.

## A helpful distinction may still require a concession

The testing question uses a nutrition-label analogy (162–171). The answer endorses
company disclosures and independent audits, but this does not establish that a
standardized label system exists. It also contains a significant tension: users
are described as able to verify mistakes (180–191), yet increasing model quality
is said to risk reducing users' own scrutiny (193–209). These claims cannot be
compressed into either universal user competence or inevitable blind reliance.

The jobs question (210–222) quotes an earlier threat formulation and suggests an
employment interpretation. The answer begins with prediction uncertainty, then
expresses optimism about future work. At 239–248, the distinction between tasks
and jobs reframes what the systems do. But at 249–270 the witness explicitly
acknowledges that some jobs will be automated and mitigation will be required.
The distinction therefore does not support a conclusion that jobs are unaffected.
Nor does optimism establish the distribution, timing or quality of future work.

The eventual worst-fear answer (283–298) broadens the scope to harm caused by the
field and industry. The questioner's proposed employment interpretation is not
adopted as the sole meaning. This scope change matters when connecting a short
preview to the full answer: it is not merely repetition of the chair's concern.

## An analogy with a retained limit

The election question (299–308) elicits a broader concern about interactive
persuasion and disinformation. Disclosure, voluntary action and regulation are
proposed. The later Photoshop analogy (338–345) suggests that people can learn
to question media authenticity, but the following clauses (346–351) emphasize
interactivity and predictive capacity as complications. The response ends with a
combination of company action, regulation and education, not public adaptation
alone. The analogy is a rhetorical comparison, not evidence of equivalent harms
or a demonstrated solution. Election-relative timing remains tied to the source.

## The follow-up exposes two meanings of democratization

The concentration question (352–366) concerns resources, corporate influence and
the conditions of competition. The first answer emphasizes input into values and
broad access to tools, while acknowledging that few companies can train frontier
models and calling for scrutiny (367–400). Access and production capacity are
already distinguishable within that answer.

The questioner pushes back (401–425), invoking failed promises of democratization
and asking how opportunity can be dispersed when model creation remains resource-
concentrated. This is evidence of resistance to the initial framing, not an
independent endorsement of it. The final answer (426–447) acknowledges questions
about who creates models and whose values they follow, but returns primarily to
what application builders can do on top of a platform.

That answer addresses downstream use. It does not fully resolve ownership,
infrastructure power or political influence. The analysis need not label this
intentional evasion to identify the mismatch: the question and answer emphasize
different dimensions. Reported affordability and innovation are also not measures
of dispersed control. The subsequent hearing close establishes no substantive
agreement on the unresolved issue.
