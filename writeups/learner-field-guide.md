# Learner field guide: communication jobs

Status: **provisional practice guide; not a validated best-practice library**.

Use this guide to choose a communication job, inspect examples, adapt a form and rehearse it. The cards are transcript-derived hypotheses. Exact phrase families show where wording was retrieved; they do not prove that the wording works, that a speaker intended a particular effect, or that repetition equals effectiveness.

## The repeatable learning loop

1. **Name the job.** Are you opening, explaining, asking, disagreeing, repairing, qualifying, closing or doing something else?
2. **State the listener problem.** Say what the listener would otherwise have to infer.
3. **Inspect, then adapt.** Read an exact transcript example and watch/listen to a delivery sample. Borrow the communicative move, not the speaker’s identity, accent, personality or mannerism.
4. **Use the form.** Change the words, stakes, culture and relationship to fit your situation.
5. **Bound the claim.** Mark evidence status, uncertainty, consent, responsibility and what the move cannot solve.
6. **Check the listener.** Ask for a restatement and what remains unclear; treat one response as feedback, not proof of general effectiveness.
7. **Revise.** Keep the useful function, remove imitation and record the non-fit case.

## Detailed practice cards

### opening

**Listener problem:** Give the listener a reason and orientation for the exchange.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: an opening can reduce orientation cost by naming the situation, relationship or question before detail arrives.

**Use this form:** Name the room or question; state why it matters; preview the route.

**Retrieved phrase families:** No direct family indexed. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Open the same two-minute explanation with context, then without it; ask a listener what they think the talk is for.

**Self-check:** Can a listener state the situation and purpose without guessing?

**Boundary:** Do not force a formal opening into a fast response or an already shared conversation.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### closing

**Listener problem:** End without leaving the main point or next step diffuse.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: a bounded close can compress the route and give the listener a retrievable takeaway, but repetition alone is not evidence of understanding.

**Use this form:** Return to the point; name the limit or next step; release the turn.

**Retrieved phrase families:** so the, ultimately, the point is, what matters, in short, the lesson, in conclusion. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Close a short explanation in one sentence, then ask a listener to restate the takeaway and what remains open.

**Self-check:** Does the close add orientation rather than merely announce that the talk is ending?

**Boundary:** Do not close over a live objection that still needs an answer.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### question

**Listener problem:** Invite inquiry, reflection or a useful next turn.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: a question can allocate attention and make the next reasoning step visible, but its usefulness depends on answerability and consent.

**Use this form:** Ask one specific question; give context; allow a genuine answer or opt-out.

**Retrieved phrase families:** ?, how, why, can we, what if. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Replace a broad question with a specific, answerable one and compare the next turn.

**Self-check:** Could the listener answer, decline or ask for clarification without penalty?

**Boundary:** Do not use a rhetorical question to disguise a demand or corner someone.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### answer_or_response

**Listener problem:** Respond to the literal prompt while addressing the underlying need.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: naming the frame and then answering can reduce mismatch between what was asked and what the listener needs, but reframing can also evade the question.

**Use this form:** Answer the literal point; state a useful frame if needed; give the bounded answer.

**Retrieved phrase families:** No direct family indexed. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Give a direct answer, then a frame-corrected answer; ask which one remains responsive.

**Self-check:** Can the listener identify both the direct answer and any justified reframing?

**Boundary:** Do not reframe merely to avoid an uncomfortable but legitimate question.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### story_or_personal_experience

**Listener problem:** Make an idea concrete without turning one life into universal proof.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: time, place and action can give an abstract point human and temporal scale; an explicit boundary can prevent overgeneralization.

**Use this form:** Give a bounded scene; say what it changed or illustrates; state what it cannot prove.

**Retrieved phrase families:** i was, when i, years ago, i remember, one day, my first, growing up. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Tell a 45-second event, then name the narrow lesson and one limit.

**Self-check:** Can the listener separate the event, the interpretation and the limit?

**Boundary:** Do not disclose another person’s private experience or use trauma as decorative evidence.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### example_or_case

**Listener problem:** Give a general claim a concrete handle without presenting an illustration as proof.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: a concrete case can make an abstract claim inspectable; returning to the claim helps distinguish illustration from evidence.

**Use this form:** State the claim; give one representative case; return to the claim and its evidence boundary.

**Retrieved phrase families:** for example, such as, for instance, one example. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Explain a principle with and without a case; ask which detail made the idea more inspectable.

**Self-check:** Does the case clarify the claim without pretending to establish its prevalence or cause?

**Boundary:** Do not select a vivid outlier to imply a typical pattern without denominators.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### contrast_or_disagreement

**Listener problem:** Make a distinction or change in reasoning visible without escalating disagreement.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: naming both sides can reduce the listener’s need to infer the replacement claim, but contrast can oversimplify real alternatives.

**Use this form:** Name the earlier view; identify what changed; state the present view and remaining overlap.

**Retrieved phrase families:** but, used to, now i, instead, however, rather than, on the other hand. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Use ‘I used to think X; Y changed the question; now I think Z,’ then test whether the distinction is fair.

**Self-check:** Can the listener state what changed without turning the other side into a caricature?

**Boundary:** Do not manufacture a false binary or treat a person as an argument to defeat.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### correction_or_reframe

**Listener problem:** Move from an unhelpful starting frame to a more useful question or category.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: an explicit frame correction can prevent downstream reasoning from inheriting a false premise, but it can feel evasive if the original concern is ignored.

**Use this form:** Acknowledge the starting frame; explain the correction; answer the more useful question.

**Retrieved phrase families:** No direct family indexed. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Answer a difficult prompt twice: first literally, then with one transparent frame correction.

**Self-check:** Did you preserve the valid concern inside the revised frame?

**Boundary:** Do not label a challenge a ‘bad question’ simply because it is inconvenient.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### repair_or_self_correction

**Listener problem:** Restore accuracy or shared understanding after an error, interruption or unclear phrase.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: a visible repair can update the listener’s model and reduce ambiguity, but excessive self-correction can interrupt the thought.

**Use this form:** Mark the error; replace it plainly; continue without over-explaining.

**Retrieved phrase families:** No direct family indexed. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Record a short answer with one intentional error and repair it in one sentence.

**Self-check:** Is the corrected version unmistakable, and can the listener continue with you?

**Boundary:** Do not use ‘repair’ theatrically to avoid responsibility for a substantive claim.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### qualification_or_boundary

**Listener problem:** State a clear point while marking uncertainty, scope or conditions.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: an explicit qualifier helps locate certainty and revision conditions, but vague hedging can hide the claim rather than calibrate it.

**Use this form:** State the main claim; name its evidence status or condition; say what would change it.

**Retrieved phrase families:** i think, maybe, perhaps, i'm not sure, it depends, not necessarily, to some extent. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Convert one absolute sentence into a bounded claim with one observable condition.

**Self-check:** Is the claim clearer and more honest, rather than simply weaker?

**Boundary:** Do not add ornamental uncertainty to a claim that should be checked directly.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### definition_or_explanation

**Listener problem:** Make an unfamiliar idea usable without overextending it.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: an operational definition reduces ambiguity; naming exclusions can prevent the listener from applying it outside its scope.

**Use this form:** Name the term; give its plain meaning; show one example and one boundary.

**Retrieved phrase families:** is when, means that, in other words, what i mean, the definition, defined as. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Define a technical term for a newcomer in 20 seconds, then ask them to use it in a new sentence.

**Self-check:** Can the listener use the term and say what it does not mean?

**Boundary:** Do not substitute a slogan, metaphor or credential for a usable definition.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### transition_or_structure

**Listener problem:** Help a listener follow a change of topic, scale, speaker or reasoning step.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: a signposted transition lowers navigation cost by showing how the next section connects, but too many labels can make speech mechanical.

**Use this form:** Close the current point; name the relationship; enter the next point.

**Retrieved phrase families:** No direct family indexed. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Add one spoken bridge between two unrelated ideas and ask a listener to describe the connection.

**Self-check:** Can the listener say why the next point arrived here?

**Boundary:** Do not use a transition to imply a connection that the evidence does not support.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### callback_or_repetition

**Listener problem:** Give a long exchange continuity or let an earlier image carry changed meaning.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: a callback can bind distant parts of a talk and make a shift noticeable, but repetition can become empty emphasis.

**Use this form:** Introduce a meaningful anchor; return to it with a new application; avoid merely repeating it.

**Retrieved phrase families:** No direct family indexed. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Use one image at the opening and return to it at the close with a changed implication.

**Self-check:** What new work does the repeated phrase do the second time?

**Boundary:** Do not repeat a slogan to pressure agreement or simulate evidence.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### humor_or_rapport

**Listener problem:** Create ease or relational room without making affected people the joke.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: self-directed or situational humor may lower social pressure and make a difficult turn more approachable, but context and power determine risk.

**Use this form:** Use lightness about your own situation or the shared setting; return clearly to the point.

**Retrieved phrase families:** No direct family indexed. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Try a self-directed release and a neutral transition after difficult material; check whether either targets someone else.

**Self-check:** Who bears the cost of the joke, and can the listener opt out of laughing?

**Boundary:** Do not use humor to trivialize grief, vulnerability, identity or an unresolved harm.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### credit_or_acknowledgment

**Listener problem:** Make contribution, support or dependence visible without turning praise into a status performance.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: specific credit can widen the authorship frame and show the work behind an outcome, but lists can exclude or tokenize people.

**Use this form:** Name the contribution; connect it to the result; state what remains owed or unfinished.

**Retrieved phrase families:** No direct family indexed. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Replace generic thanks with one observable contribution and its consequence.

**Self-check:** Could the credited person recognize the description as accurate and specific?

**Boundary:** Do not claim to transfer authority through praise while retaining all control.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### call_to_action_or_audience_role

**Listener problem:** Turn a broad aspiration into a voluntary, bounded next role.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: naming action, owner, time horizon and limit can make a request evaluable, but a request is not proof that the action is feasible or effective.

**Use this form:** Name the problem; offer a specific action and responsible actor; state the limit and consent path.

**Retrieved phrase families:** don't, you can, let's, do not, we need to, join us, take action. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Rewrite ‘we should care’ as one feasible action with an owner, timeframe and non-fit.

**Self-check:** Can the listener say what they may do and what the action cannot solve?

**Boundary:** Do not assign risk, money or care labor without authority, consent or support.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

### persuasion_or_advocacy

**Listener problem:** Move a room toward judgment or action while showing evidence, tradeoffs and responsibility.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: a visible chain from problem to mechanism to responsibility can make advocacy inspectable, but rhetorical force does not establish policy truth or outcomes.

**Use this form:** State the stake; show the mechanism; name tradeoffs and accountable actors; invite a bounded choice.

**Retrieved phrase families:** No direct family indexed. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Make one advocacy claim with evidence status, opposing concern, responsible actor and review point.

**Self-check:** Can a listener disagree with the proposal while accurately restating it?

**Boundary:** Do not use urgency, fear or moral identity to conceal missing evidence or coercion.

### reflection_or_value_frame

**Listener problem:** Connect an event or decision to a value without pretending the value settles the facts.

**Why it might help:** Hypothesis: an explicit value frame can help a listener understand why a detail matters, but moral language can overrule evidence or disagreement.

**Use this form:** Describe the event; name the value it raises; distinguish interpretation from verified fact.

**Retrieved phrase families:** No direct family indexed. These are search evidence, not scripts.

**Practice:** Take one personal or public event and state the value interpretation plus one alternative reading.

**Self-check:** Can the listener tell which part is observation, value judgment and open question?

**Boundary:** Do not use a value label to end inquiry or claim moral superiority.

**Evidence status:** 8 transcript examples and 2 bounded delivery samples; independent review and outcome evidence remain pending.

## Delivery inspection questions

When watching or listening to a sample, record observations rather than explanations:

- **Voice and timing:** Where does the speaker slow, speed up, pause, restart or overlap? Is the observation based on listening or only on a transcript/proxy?
- **Visible action:** Is the speaker visible continuously? What posture, gaze, gesture or facial movement is actually observable, and what is missing?
- **Setting and edit:** Is this a podium, interview, staged scene, cutaway, music bed or subtitle edit? Could the edit explain the apparent effect?
- **Alignment:** Does the retained wording clearly belong to the visible speaker at that moment? Use `indeterminate` when it does not.
- **Accessibility:** Could the cue be replaced with a textual, captioned, audio-only or asynchronous practice route? Do not require eye contact, facial expression, recording or disclosure.

## Safety and evidence boundary

Do not copy a speaker’s identity, accent, personality, status performance or mannerism. Do not treat an emotional image, vocal intensity, proxy measurement, applause, popularity or repeated phrase as proof of truth, comprehension, persuasion or effectiveness. Keep source identity, recurrence, delivery interpretation, learner teachability and listener outcomes as separate gates.
