# Amazing presentation patterns from the corpus

## What this is

This is the practical presentation edition of the corpus pattern mining. It
collects the recurring themes, subthemes and structural moves that are most
useful when building a talk.

The current repository’s authoritative corpus is **395 full-context reviews**
from a **480-video inventory**. This document uses that available corpus; it
does not claim that the corpus currently contains 420 fully reviewed videos.
The counts below are discovery counts, not scores of quality or proof that a
pattern causes audience success.

For the broader fixed-inventory recovery layer, see the [full-inventory pattern
candidates](full-inventory-pattern-candidates.md) and [coverage audit](corpus-coverage-audit.md).
That layer adds 71 direct timestamped machine-transcript candidates, 11
alternate-source candidates and one externally hosted transcript candidate,
bringing unique text-bearing coverage to 478/480 while preserving the
distinction from the 395 project-generated, single-pass full-context records,
which are not independent human validation. The two remaining IDs are
source-unavailable (one removed and one private), so they are not represented
as transcript evidence.

The basic rule is:

> Borrow the communication structure, then rewrite it in your own evidence,
> voice, audience relationship and level of certainty.

For a card-by-card drafting workflow across all 55 candidates, use the
[presentation pattern playbook](presentation-pattern-playbook.html) or its
[written version](presentation-pattern-playbook.md). Each card turns the
candidate form into draft moves, a practice check, a source anchor and a
non-imitation boundary.

For the human-review order of these candidates, use the [pattern review-priority
map](presentation-pattern-review-priorities.md). It orders workload by corpus
coverage and recovery-quality flags; it is not an effectiveness ranking.

## The 22 core themes

### 1. Orient before detail

**Form:** situation → stake → route.  
**Found in:** 147 videos.

Use this at the beginning of a presentation, section or answer. Tell people
where they are, why the point matters, and how you will get there. Do not open
with background that has no visible purpose.

### 2. Make a concrete scene carry an idea

**Form:** bounded scene → interpretation → what the scene cannot prove.  
**Found in:** 170 videos.

Use one specific moment instead of a pile of abstractions. After the story,
state the lesson explicitly; then prevent the anecdote from pretending to be
general evidence.

### 3. Use contrast to show change

**Form:** earlier frame → what changed → present view → genuine overlap.  
**Found in:** 167 videos.

Use this for before/after, disagreement, learning or a change of plan. Show
the earlier position fairly. Contrast should clarify a difference, not create a
false enemy.

### 4. Make an analogy do controlled work

**Form:** introduce an image → map the relevant relation → state where it stops.  
**Found in:** 167 videos.

Use an analogy to make an unfamiliar idea inspectable. Explain exactly which
relationship transfers and where the comparison breaks.

### 5. Turn hope into action

**Form:** aspiration → bounded next action → limit or condition.  
**Found in:** 189 videos.

Use this near the end of a persuasive talk. Replace vague inspiration with one
action, an owner, available support and a realistic boundary.

### 6. Invite the audience into a safe next turn

**Form:** specific invitation → answerable response → safe decline or opt-out.  
**Found in:** 196 videos.

Use it for questions, discussion, audience participation or a call to action.
Make the requested response concrete and do not use participation pressure as a
substitute for clarity.

### 7. Move between scales

**Form:** personal example → institutional condition → collective implication,
with an explicit bridge.  
**Found in:** 165 videos.

Use this when a talk needs to connect an individual story to policy, culture or
shared responsibility. Say when you are changing scale so the audience can
follow the reasoning.

### 8. Qualify without weakening the point

**Form:** claim → condition, scope or evidence limit.  
**Found in:** 163 videos.

Use this when certainty is limited or the audience may overgeneralize. Put the
main point in plain language, then state what would change it or where it does
not apply.

### 9. Turn credit into collective agency

**Form:** contribution → result → people and conditions that enabled it.  
**Found in:** 158 videos.

Use this when acknowledging a team, community or predecessor. Name specific
contributions without pretending that praise itself proves the result.

### 10. Make responsibility precise

**Form:** decision owner → available support → remaining responsibility.  
**Found in:** 154 videos.

Use it when a talk asks someone to act. Distinguish what the speaker can do,
what another actor controls, and what help is needed.

### 11. Revalue the metric

**Form:** familiar measure → what it misses → human-relevant measure.  
**Found in:** 153 videos.

Use it when numbers are dominating the conversation. Keep the useful part of
the metric, expose its blind spot, and explain the better decision criterion.

### 12. Answer the useful question

**Form:** surface question → deeper decision-relevant question → answer and
condition.  
**Found in:** 149 videos.

Use it for interviews, hostile questions and confusing prompts. Acknowledge
the literal question before reframing; otherwise the audience may experience
the answer as evasion.

### 13. Treat objections fairly

**Form:** strongest version of objection → response → remaining uncertainty.  
**Found in:** 149 videos.

Use this in proposals and disagreement. Let the audience recognize their own
concern in your version before answering it.

### 14. Make identity relevant, not evidentiary

**Form:** explain why identity/status affects the situation → connect it to the
claim → do not use identity as proof.  
**Found in:** 141 videos.

Use lived experience to establish perspective and stakes, not automatic
authority over every factual question.

### 15. Separate values from facts

**Form:** factual observation → value judgment → decision or implication.  
**Found in:** 123 videos.

Use this in moral, political or organizational presentations. Mark which part
is evidence and which part is the value you are asking the audience to share or
debate.

### 16. Define, exemplify, bound

**Form:** plain definition → concrete example → boundary or counterexample.  
**Found in:** 117 videos.

Use this for technical or unfamiliar concepts. The example makes the term
usable; the boundary prevents it from becoming an empty slogan.

### 17. Name risk before asking for trust

**Form:** risk/failure mode → mitigation or condition → responsible action.  
**Found in:** 110 videos.

Use it before a recommendation, commitment or high-stakes request. Audiences
usually trust a speaker more when the downside is not hidden.

### 18. Explain how a change could lead to a result

**Form:** claim → steps that could make it happen → evidence still needed.  
**Found in:** 97 videos.

Use this when explaining why you think a change may lead to a result. Show the
steps in between, and say plainly when those steps have not yet been checked.

### 19. Connect personal experience to systems

**Form:** one person's experience → rules or conditions around them → choices
that remain.  
**Found in:** 91 videos.

Use this when a problem is not just about one person's effort. Explain the
rules or conditions around them, while showing what choices they still have.

### 20. Repair yourself cleanly

**Form:** mark the error → replace it plainly → continue.  
**Found in:** 70 videos.

Use this when you misstate a fact, overclaim or answer the wrong question.
Short, visible correction is stronger than defensive explanation.

### 21. Hold loss and continuation together

**Form:** name the loss → refuse false resolution → identify what care or work
continues.  
**Found in:** 69 videos.

Use this in memorial, crisis or change presentations. Do not rush grief into an
inspirational lesson.

### 22. Use lightness, then return to substance

**Form:** brief self-aware joke or shared observation → reconnect with the room
→ main point.  
**Found in:** 45 videos.

Use humor to make the room feel more at ease, not to dodge responsibility, tell
someone else's private story or make another person the joke. Then return to
your point.

## The 18 high-value structural subthemes

These are smaller architectures that can be inserted inside the core themes.

| Subtheme | Use it when | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| Premise correction | The audience is asking from a misleading starting point | acknowledge premise → missing distinction → corrected answer |
| Scope repair | A claim is too broad | identify overreach → narrow → preserve valid core |
| Analogy transfer | A difficult idea needs a familiar handle | source image → relevant relation → stopping point |
| Evidence gap | You need to be persuasive without overclaiming | claim → missing evidence → next test |
| Expectation reversal | The audience expects one outcome but reality turns | expected frame → observed turn → consequence |
| Register shift | The room needs a change in tone or complexity | signal shift → keep point stable → return to shared frame |
| Audience shift | Different listeners need different explanations | identify audience → change level → name new role |
| Credit redirection | Praise is landing on one person | receive praise → widen causal chain → name contributors |
| Hands-off responsibility | You do not control the decision | state limit → identify owner → offer support |
| Reciprocal learning | You want dialogue rather than performance | observation → invite correction → update shared account |
| Self-undercut | Your stake or limitation could distort the claim | name limitation → state claim → give check |
| Despair to hope | The audience feels the problem is closed | preserve difficulty → identify possibility → bounded action |
| Metric revaluation | The visible metric is driving the wrong behavior | familiar metric → blind spot → better measure |
| Moral to memorial | A disputed judgment needs human significance | judgment → human meaning → no false proof |
| Optional support | The audience may be vulnerable or burdened | name need → offer choice → preserve opt-out |
| Question redefinition | The literal question hides the decision | repeat surface question → explain limit → ask useful question |
| Sorrow and continuation | Loss must be acknowledged without closure theatre | name loss → refuse resolution → name continuing care |
| Story to thesis | A story needs to become a presentation point | bounded scene → explicit lesson → limit |

## Fifteen additional subthemes

These emerged from labels that did not fit the first taxonomy. They are useful
creative leads and should be tested in context before being treated as stable
patterns.

| Subtheme | Practical use |
|---|---|
| Purpose → attention → example | Tell the audience why the exchange matters, give one example, return to the purpose. |
| Recognition → specific credit | Turn general praise into a named contribution and result. |
| Unfinished work → commitment | Admit what is incomplete, state the commitment and name the next accountable step. |
| Familiarity → scope | Start with a familiar setting, widen carefully and mark where the analogy ends. |
| Unity across scale | Name a shared value while preserving real disagreement. |
| Authority → service | Show what a role controls, then return agency to affected people. |
| Difficulty → capability | Name the difficulty, show the support or response, identify the capability built. |
| Invitation → low-barrier entry | Make participation concrete and offer a first step that does not require full commitment. |
| Access obstacle → support | Name the barrier, offer support and preserve the recipient’s choice. |
| Emotion → composure | Name difficulty, choose a controllable response and continue without pretending the feeling vanished. |
| Selection → incentive | State the selection criterion, show the choice and explain what the incentive changes. |
| Failure → reframe | Separate a failed outcome from identity and choose the next experiment. |
| Authenticity versus performance | Separate lived claim from performed form and explain what remains constructed. |
| Craft process over outcome | Show choices and constraints instead of presenting the final result as magic. |
| Technical explanation → interruption | Pause the technical thread, translate the key point, answer the interruption, then resume. |

## Six complete presentation architectures

Use these as starting blueprints, then fill them with the themes above.

1. **Explain an unfamiliar idea:** orientation → definition → concrete example →
   analogy → boundary → listener question.
2. **Make a proposal:** problem → stakes → options → objection → risk limit →
   bounded action and owner.
3. **Report an incident or failure:** situation → expectation → observed turn →
   impact → repair → next experiment.
4. **Lead a room through change:** earlier frame → evidence of change → what
   remains true → roles and support → next step.
5. **Answer a hard question:** acknowledge premise → direct answer → useful
   reframe → condition/evidence gap → invitation to check.
6. **Tell a story with a point:** bounded scene → tension or contrast → meaning
   made explicit → limit of the story → transferable choice.

## How to use the atlas to make a presentation

1. Write the communication job in one sentence: “I need the audience to
   understand/decide/feel/try ___.”
2. Choose one primary architecture and no more than three supporting themes.
3. Fill each move with your own evidence, example or decision. Do not copy the
   source speaker’s wording, personality or biography.
4. Add one qualification, one counterexample and one non-fit warning.
5. Draft a listener check: “What changed? What is the main point? What remains
   uncertain? What would you do next?”
6. Rehearse two versions while changing only one delivery choice—pace, pause,
   emphasis, visual, or turn-taking.
7. Ask a consenting listener to restate the point and identify pressure,
   ambiguity or unnecessary burden. Revise from that evidence.

## What not to infer

These patterns are source-linked candidate structures. Their recurrence does
not prove that they caused audience understanding, trust, memory or action.
The corpus also cannot establish a speaker’s private intention from words or
visual proxies alone. Use the patterns as disciplined drafting tools and test
them with real audiences.
