## 1. Rowan Atkinson: the same argument uses different kinds of support

The address opens by ranking free expression alongside basic needs, then
acknowledges the speaker's claimed protection from public visibility. That
standpoint leads to concern for people with less visibility. The account does
not claim the speaker personally shares every risk attributed to those people.

Around [01:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1qfB9wExiy8&t=72s), three reported
arrest cases lead into an explicitly fictional comedy sketch. The comparison
supports an absurdity argument, but the sketch is not a fourth observed legal
case. A later imagined newspaper-to-police exchange is another distinct type:
it illustrates a proposed mechanism rather than documenting correspondence.

| Support form in the speech | What this reading can establish | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| Reported arrest cases | The speaker offers them as examples | Case accuracy or representativeness |
| Fictional sketch | An explicitly fictional comparison is made | Another real-world occurrence |
| Attributed committee statement | Institutional authority is invoked | Exact quotation or current law |
| Exposure/immunity analogy | A comparison explains a proposed principle | Medical or psychological validity |
| Imagined media/police exchange | A possible pressure sequence is dramatized | Actual motives or correspondence |

### A counterargument is answered with a competing explanation

At [02:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1qfB9wExiy8&t=162s), a reported
defense treats dropped cases as evidence that the law works. The speaker instead
attributes dismissal to publicity, adds the costs of the process and raises

It does not independently establish that publicity caused dismissal or how many

### A reframe changes where the problem is located

Around [12:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4u71aIcrxtQ&t=765s), the response
challenges a formulation about finding women's voices. It treats voice as
already present and redirects attention toward being able to use it and being

A subsequent request for project details receives an explicit refusal to disclose

It should not be marked as a fully explained plan, nor should missing details
be supplied by the analyst.

### The requested resolution never appears

At [20:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4u71aIcrxtQ&t=1216s), the interviewer
asks about disagreements and then requests an example of how the last one was
resolved. The exchange supplies uncertainty and broad comments about differences,

This is a useful negative case. An agreeable ending is not proof that the
question was answered. Later comments about finding common ground in charity

They do not retroactively supply the missing resolution.

### Awareness, exposure and impact stay separate

Participants repeatedly describe campaign success, and the interviewer sometimes
presupposes it. One participant recalls news coverage in one location, then
speculates about effects elsewhere. Those are reported exposure and interpretation,
not independent measurements of global reach, changed stigma or clinical benefit.
Speaker descriptions of nodding or laughter are also not directly observed here.

## What changes in the research method

Every example should carry its support type. Every answer should be checked
against what was actually asked. This batch provides distinctions to apply in
later readings:

- documented-in-this-corpus evidence versus a speaker's reported case;
- fictional illustration versus hypothetical mechanism;
- retrospective claim versus prospective scenario;
- bounded disclosure versus complete answer;
- general agreement versus a demonstrated resolution;
- reported exposure versus independently measured outcome.

These are analytic distinctions, not new proven best practices. No legacy labels,
practice-card admissions or pause judgments change. Genre, turn attribution,
source authenticity and audience outcomes need separate verification before
cross-speaker recurrence or effectiveness claims can be strengthened.
