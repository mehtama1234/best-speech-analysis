## Pattern cards
### A. Refuse the quote but name the conduct
Do not repeat harmful language for shock value. State what happened, why it matters and what standard it violates. **Reusable form:** “I will not repeat the words. The conduct was ____, it used power this way, and it cannot be treated as normal.”
### B. Break the normal script openly
Say why the planned speech cannot continue unchanged. The admission makes the interruption legible and tells the audience that the interruption is part of the message.
### C. Replace a minimizing label with a precise frame
List the names people use to shrink the issue, then explain what those labels hide: power, pattern, consent, fear and consequences.
### D. Move from headline to body memory
Translate a public statement into street, workplace and assault experiences that listeners may recognize. Do this without demanding that survivors disclose their stories or relive trauma.
### E. Name the silence pressure
Explain why people minimize harm: fear of not being believed, being called weak, or disturbing a powerful person. Naming the pressure can make room for speech without making disclosure an obligation.
### F. Remove party labels from a human boundary
When the issue is safety or dignity, address people across political identity. The move widens the audience, but it must not pretend that policies, power or responsibility are politically identical.
### G. Widen the impact to children and future models
Ask what children learn from public behavior. This gives the audience a longer horizon and includes boys and girls without treating children as political props.
### H. Use a child’s rule as moral compression
A simple child observation can carry the adult standard in one line. Label it as an illustration, not proof of how every child thinks.
### I. Define strength by what it does to others
Move from dominance to lifting, joining and healing. A positive definition gives the audience a behavior to look for rather than only an identity to reject.
### J. Turn teaching into a leadership test
Take values adults teach children—education, service, teamwork, persistence—and ask whether a leader’s record shows those values. This makes endorsement a case rather than a slogan.
### K. Build character through accumulation
List roles, choices and responses to setbacks. The force comes from the pattern across time, not from one credential or one flattering story.
### L. Close with a replacement, not only condemnation
After naming what must stop, describe the behavior and leadership the audience should expect. The contrast creates a forward path without softening the harm.
## Product implications
1. **Harm-without-repetition editor:** help speakers describe abuse, harassment or degrading speech without replaying harmful words or sensational detail.
2. **Script-interruption coach:** identify when a new event requires a speaker to abandon prepared remarks and explain that choice clearly.
3. **Minimization-to-conduct translator:** convert labels such as “banter” into conduct, power, consent, pattern and consequence questions.
4. **Headline-to-experience map:** connect a public claim to possible street, workplace, school and family effects while avoiding forced disclosure.
5. **Silence-pressure checklist:** surface fear of disbelief, retaliation, shame, power imbalance and emotional cost before asking for testimony.
6. **Cross-party human-boundary builder:** state a shared dignity rule, then keep policy, power and accountability differences visible.
7. **Child-impact lens:** ask what girls, boys and young people may learn from a public example, with child-safety and developmental review.
8. **Illustration-versus-proof labeler:** mark anecdotes as examples and require evidence before treating them as broad facts.
9. **Strength-definition coach:** replace dominance language with observable acts such as lifting others, joining people and repairing harm.
10. **Values-to-record builder:** map the values taught to children against a candidate or leader’s actions, roles, decisions and failures.
11. **Cumulative-character timeline:** organize evidence across time, distinguish verified records from claims, and show gaps rather than creating a halo.
12. **Condemnation-to-standard close:** move from what must stop to the behavior, safeguards and leadership standard the audience should demand.
13. **Trauma-aware speech mode:** warn about graphic detail, offer content notes, avoid survivor pressure and point toward support without pretending the speech is therapy.

## Limits and next test
This is partisan campaign speech delivered during a charged election. Its claims about the candidate, public records, women’s experiences, children and political leadership require independent source review. Sexual-violence material can be distressing; a speech product must not pressure survivors to disclose or use trauma as persuasion. The child anecdote is illustrative, not representative evidence. The positive image of men and leadership should not erase women’s accounts or turn “decency” into a substitute for policy and accountability.
