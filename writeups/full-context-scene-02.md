## What the excerpt constructs

The sequence is not simply a motivational slogan. A teacher selects a reader,
has a poem read, names its sentiment, elicits a translation, rejects one proposed
explanation, supplies a mortality rationale, directs attention to predecessors,
and returns to the phrase as an instruction. The words change function through
the sequence: first an object of explanation, later an injunction to the students.

```text
Directed reading → named sentiment → translation and feedback
  → why-question → mortality rationale → comparison with predecessors
  → imagined legacy voice → imperative and broad aspiration
```

This is a scripted film scene. Its performed exchanges can illustrate how a
scene represents teaching; they do not establish how real students learn or
respond. Complete retained-excerpt coverage is not complete film coverage.

## Participation has different functions

At 0–10, the teacher selects a named student and specifies a reading task.
The retained text includes the poem title and confirmation. The following
reading at 11–14 is partially repeated and linked to a Latin sentiment at 15–17.
Borrowed literary language supplies the lesson's material, not independent
evidence that its advice works.

At 18–22, a translation is elicited and approved. At 23–25, the question changes
from meaning to explanation: a hurry-based response is rejected. The difference
matters. The scene does not show that translating the phrase amounts to
understanding the teacher's rationale. Nor does a rejection prove that a real
student learned from the feedback. Named replies and feedback are part of the
script, not independent observations from an educational study.

## Mortality becomes a local comparison

At 26–30, the teacher supplies mortality as the reason for urgency. At 31–35,
students are instructed to inspect faces from the past that they have allegedly
passed without attending to. Repeated similarities at 36–44 connect those
predecessors to the current students: appearance, youth and aspirations. The
question at 44–46 introduces potential left unrealized; it is not answered by
biographical evidence about the pictured people.

The account of the predecessors' death at 47–48 reinforces the rationale.
The comparison makes a general proposition locally imaginable, but does not
demonstrate that everyone depicted failed to realize their potential. That would
be an unwarranted inference from the question.

The instructions to approach and look are text evidence of requested actions.
They do not verify students moving, a particular gaze direction, camera framing
or the props actually visible. Those require video inspection.

## Candidate pattern and limits

The candidate is a **return with a changed function**: a phrase is first explained,
then supported with a rationale and local comparison, then reused as a direct
instruction. This differs from counting every repetition as an equivalent
emphasis event. Intervening material changes what the repeated phrase is doing.

A practice hypothesis would be to introduce an idea, make its rationale concrete,
and return to it with a clear requested action. This example's final request is
not operationally specific, and its mortality framing may not suit every audience.
Neither that exercise nor the scene establishes a universal best practice.
