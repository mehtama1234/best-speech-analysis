# Draft lesson: make the comparison explicit

Status: draft practice hypothesis; not learner-validated.

## Communication job

Use a clear comparison when the listener needs to understand what changed,
which expectation was wrong, or why a proposed distinction matters.

## What the evidence currently supports

The reviewed packet contains textual contrasts across personal reconsideration,
normative rejection, narrative correction and concession/correction. It also
contains controls where a speech works through a takeaway, example, story or
qualification without explicit contrast. A repeated word such as “but” is not
enough: one selected segment is an incomplete caption fragment, and another
uses “but” for a personal-story transition rather than disagreement.

## Practice task

Choose a low-stakes explanation. Record two versions:

1. State the expected or commonly assumed position in one sentence.
2. State what the example, observation or decision changes in one sentence.
3. Name the consequence: what should the listener now understand or do?
4. Add one uncertainty or limit if the evidence does not settle the issue.

Template:

> “The expectation is **[A]**. In this case, **[B]**. The important change is
> **[consequence]**. This does not establish **[limit]**.”

Do not manufacture an opponent. If the change is in your own reasoning, say so.
If the job is only to tell a story, give an example, qualify a prediction or
close a talk, use the simpler form instead of forcing a contrast.

## Self-check

- Did I state both sides of the comparison?
- Is the difference grounded in an observation, example or decision?
- Did I distinguish a change in reasoning from interpersonal disagreement?
- Did I explain why the difference matters?
- Did I avoid treating “but,” “instead” or “wrong” as magic words?
- Did I name uncertainty, missing context or an alternative explanation?
- Would the explanation remain clear without dramatic delivery?

## Safety and accessibility boundary

Do not use contrast to create a false binary, assign motives, intensify conflict,
or turn a person into the wrong side of an argument. Do not require eye contact,
gesture, vocal force or rapid rebuttal. A written comparison, captioned example,
pause, visual table or additional processing time may perform the same job.

## Learner check

Give the learner a new transcript segment and ask them to identify A, B, the
consequence and the limit. Then ask them to produce a two-sentence comparison
on a new topic and to label whether the segment is actually a contrast, a story
transition, an example, a qualification or a conclusion. A second listener
should restate the intended change without being told the answer.

This check measures recognition, transfer, misuse detection and clarity of the
lesson. It does not establish that contrast improves trust, memory or action.
