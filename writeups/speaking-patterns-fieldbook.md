# The Speaking Patterns Fieldbook

## A practical guide to speaking clearly

Seven chapters address common speaking problems. The index groups 55 ideas into 22 main themes, 18 smaller speaking moves and 15 more ideas to explore. Each entry links to its example and practice task. These are ideas to try, not proven rules.

The examples in this book come from 17 selected transcript analyses. The larger project has reviewed the full transcripts of 395 of the 480 videos in its inventory. This book does not recheck all 395. Other reviewers have not checked these readings, and no audience study has tested the advice. Source examples summarize transcripts; they are not exact quotations. Caption numbers mark text order, not video time.

## Start with the question your audience needs to answer

Start by writing the question your audience should be able to answer after your talk. Perhaps they need to understand a problem, compare options or make a decision. That question will help you choose what to include—and what to leave out.

Here is a made-up example. You are asking a committee to approve a public workshop. A topic such as ‘our community project’ leaves you with too much to cover. A question gives you a useful limit: ‘Should we approve one workshop next month?’ Now you know you need to explain its purpose, the staff time it requires and what the committee would be agreeing to.

That question also helps you edit. A story about a visitor may explain why the workshop is needed. A list of every event you have held may add little to this decision. For each section, ask: ‘What does this help the committee decide?’ Keep the details needed to answer that question.

Choose the chapter that fits a problem in your draft. Read the example and the note about what it cannot show. Try the exercise in your own words and use facts you can support. The index helps you find related ideas; you do not need to use every one.

## Separate the source, the lesson and the practice example

Each source passage is a summary of a transcript, not the speaker's exact words. It does not independently confirm every event described. Caption numbers show where to find a passage in the transcript; they are not times in the video. Video links open at the start times saved with the captions. The recordings were not checked here to confirm the captions or timing. The linked notes point out gaps. A transcript cannot tell us what a speaker privately meant or what listeners thought.

The chapter gives our reading of the speaker's choice and a way you could try a similar choice. Practice examples are made up and marked as such. They did not come from the videos.

Do not make a claim bigger than its evidence. One story shows what happened in one account, not how often it happens. A comparison can show one similarity, not prove that two things work the same in every way. A request shows what the speaker asked for, not whether anyone agreed.

## Seven speaking jobs

### Help the audience know where they are going

**Ask:** What does the audience need to understand first, and what will you cover next?

#### Main point

Tell listeners what the talk is about, why it matters and what you will cover. Then cover what you promised. If the plan changes, say so. End by answering the opening question or stating what remains unknown.

#### A way to do it

1. Write the audience’s main question in one sentence.
2. Name the result, decision or explanation the talk can actually provide.
3. Preview only the sections you have time to deliver; signal a change of direction when it happens.
4. Close by answering the opening question or naming what remains unresolved.

### Make an unfamiliar idea easier to picture

**Ask:** What does the audience need to picture, compare or understand?

#### Main point

Explain the idea without making it seem simpler or more certain than it is. Define the term, give a specific example and say how it helps. If you use a comparison, state what is similar and where the comparison stops.

#### A way to do it

1. Name the idea in ordinary words.
2. Give one example the audience can inspect.
3. Say which part of the example illustrates the idea.
4. State what the example cannot establish; add a second example if one case could mislead.

### Use a story to show what changed

**Ask:** What did someone expect, what happened, and what does that change mean now?

#### Main point

Use a story when it helps explain a choice, result or change in belief. Say what the person expected, what happened and what they learned. Keep the lesson within what this one account can show: it cannot tell us how often the same thing happens or what everyone should do.

#### A way to do it

1. Set the scene and the original expectation.
2. Show the choice or event that interrupted it.
3. Explain what changed—and what stayed true.
4. Limit the lesson to this case unless other evidence supports a broader claim.

### Answer hard questions without dodging them

**Ask:** What does the question assume, and what does the person asking need to decide?

#### Main point

Start by showing that you heard the question. Answer it directly when the facts allow. If the question leaves out a difference that matters, name it and explain why. Correct your own wording if it was too broad. Do not use a different question to avoid the one you were asked.

#### A way to do it

1. Restate the concern briefly and fairly.
2. Give the clearest supported answer first.
3. Name any condition, missing fact or distinction that changes the answer.
4. If you overstated something, correct the exact phrase and continue.

### Show who did the work and who owns the next step

**Ask:** Who contributed, who can decide, and what support or responsibility remains?

#### Main point

Credit and responsibility are related but different. Name the work people actually did. Say who can make the next decision and what help they need. A personal story is not a substitute for a service, policy or named person responsible for the work. If no one measured the result, say so.

#### A way to do it

1. Name specific contributions rather than praising an unnamed group.
2. Separate what is already done from what is being requested.
3. Say who decides, what help they need and who takes over any remaining work.
4. Keep reported results separate from verified results.

### Build connection without putting people on the spot

**Ask:** What relationship do your words create, and does the audience have a real choice?

#### Main point

A story, joke, identity or personal disclosure can help people see why a subject matters. It can also expose someone, reduce a group to a stereotype or pressure listeners to agree. Share only what is yours to share. Make your point without asking listeners to reveal private experiences or show a particular emotion.

#### A way to do it

1. State the point in words; do not make a gesture, identity or joke carry it alone.
2. Use a personal example only with a clear reason and a fair boundary.
3. Make participation optional and offer a way to pass or respond privately.
4. Check who bears the risk if the joke or disclosure is misunderstood.

### Ask for a next step people can actually take

**Ask:** What action is possible for this audience, with the time, authority and support they have?

#### Main point

A useful request tells people what to do, who is responsible, what support is available and what happens if the condition is not met. Match the request to the audience’s actual role. Leave room to decline, ask for more information or choose another path. A strong closing can clarify a decision; it cannot prove that the decision was made or that the proposal worked.

#### A way to do it

1. Name one action, not a cloud of good intentions.
2. Identify the person or group that can take it and the resources required.
3. State the time limit, condition, opt-out or alternative path.
4. Say how you will check what happened without treating attendance or applause as success.

## 22 broad themes

These are the 22 main themes in the larger pattern writeup. Each link opens its related detailed entry in the 55-pattern index. The counts mean a transcript search found a match in that many videos; the matches have not all been checked.

### 1. Start with context before details

**In simple terms:** Set the scene → say why it matters → show where you are going.

**Search matches:** 147 videos. These matches have not all been checked.

Use this at the beginning of a presentation, section or answer. Tell people where they are, why the point matters, and how you will get there. Do not open with background that has no visible purpose.

Related detailed pattern: [Tell listeners where the talk is going, then follow that plan](#pattern-orientation_and_roadmap).

### 2. Use one specific story to explain an idea

**In simple terms:** Tell one specific moment → explain what it shows → say what it cannot prove.

**Search matches:** 170 videos. These matches have not all been checked.

Use one specific moment instead of a pile of abstractions. After the story, state the lesson explicitly; then prevent the anecdote from pretending to be general evidence.

Related detailed pattern: [Use one scene to explain your point](#pattern-story_memory_and_scene).

### 3. Show what changed

**In simple terms:** Say what you thought → what changed → what you think now → what still holds.

**Search matches:** 167 videos. These matches have not all been checked.

Use this for before/after, disagreement, learning or a change of plan. Show the earlier position fairly. Contrast should clarify a difference, not create a false enemy.

Related detailed pattern: [Explain what changed and what stayed the same](#pattern-contrast_and_reversal).

### 4. Use an analogy carefully

**In simple terms:** Give a comparison → say what matches → say where it stops matching.

**Search matches:** 167 videos. These matches have not all been checked.

Use an analogy to make an unfamiliar idea inspectable. Explain exactly which relationship transfers and where the comparison breaks.

Related detailed pattern: [Return to an earlier image to add meaning](#pattern-analogy_metaphor_and_callback).

### 5. Turn hope into a specific next step

**In simple terms:** Say what you hope for → name one doable step → say what help or limits matter.

**Search matches:** 189 videos. These matches have not all been checked.

Use this near the end of a persuasive talk. Replace vague inspiration with one action, an owner, available support and a realistic boundary.

Related detailed pattern: [Give hope a next action, not a guaranteed ending](#pattern-future_hope_and_action).

### 6. Invite a clear response without pressure

**In simple terms:** Ask for a clear response → make it easy to answer → make it okay to decline.

**Search matches:** 196 videos. These matches have not all been checked.

Use it for questions, discussion, audience participation or a call to action. Make the requested response concrete and do not use participation pressure as a substitute for clarity.

Related detailed pattern: [Return the question to the people who can act on it](#pattern-audience_invitation_and_next_turn).

### 7. Connect one person's experience to the bigger picture

**In simple terms:** Start with one person's experience → explain the larger rule or system → connect them.

**Search matches:** 165 videos. These matches have not all been checked.

Use this when a talk needs to connect an individual story to policy, culture or shared responsibility. Say when you are changing scale so the audience can follow the reasoning.

Related detailed pattern: [Connect the total to one person's experience](#pattern-scale_shift).

### 8. State your point and its limits

**In simple terms:** Make your point → say when it applies and where it does not.

**Search matches:** 163 videos. These matches have not all been checked.

Use this when certainty is limited or the audience may overgeneralize. Put the main point in plain language, then state what would change it or where it does not apply.

Related detailed pattern: [State your point and its limits](#pattern-qualification_and_boundary).

### 9. Name everyone's contribution

**In simple terms:** Name the work people did → say what it led to → name the support behind it.

**Search matches:** 158 videos. These matches have not all been checked.

Use this when acknowledging a team, community or predecessor. Name specific contributions without pretending that praise itself proves the result.

### 10. Say who is responsible for what

**In simple terms:** Name who decides → what help is available → who is still responsible.

**Search matches:** 154 videos. These matches have not all been checked.

Use it when a talk asks someone to act. Distinguish what the speaker can do, what another actor controls, and what help is needed.

### 11. Choose a measure that reflects what matters

**In simple terms:** State the concern fairly → answer it → say what remains uncertain.

## 33 smaller patterns

The first 18 are smaller speaking moves that can fit inside a larger theme. The next 15 are additional ideas worth exploring. Each links to a full entry with a made-up example, a practice task and limits. These are not assigned to just one of the 22 themes because they can serve more than one.

### 18 smaller moves that fit inside a talk

#### Correct the assumption before supplying the detail

**Try this when:** A question asks for details of something that did not happen or a practice you do not follow. Answering inside that frame would quietly confirm the mistaken premise.

**How to use it:** Write down the fact the question assumes. Check whether you know it is true. If it is false, begin with the correction, then answer the concern the question raises. If you do not know, say what you can confirm and what still needs checking. Avoid guessing why the person asked.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-premise_correction).

#### Correct the broad claim wherever it appears

**Try this when:** You correct an overstatement in one sentence, but the slide, summary or recommendation still carries the broader claim. Readers taking away only those parts receive the error rather than the repair.

**How to use it:** After correcting a broad claim, check the title, chart labels, summary and recommendation for the old version. Ask whether you changed from attempts to people, one group to everyone, or one time period to all time. Repeat the accurate version wherever a reader might copy it. Explain the correction if it changes a decision.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-scope_repair).

#### Use one part of an analogy, not the whole story

**Try this when:** An analogy joins two situations, but the audience may carry over more than the comparison supports. A familiar example becomes an implied claim that the experiences are equivalent.

**How to use it:** Write the source situation, the target situation and the exact relationship they share. Explain the target using that relationship, then state one important difference. If the analogy concerns another person's painful experience, do not presume it is welcome or equivalent to your chosen comparison. Often the more respectful choice is not to use it.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-analogy_transfer).

#### Show where the explanation stops short of proof

**Try this when:** A comparison makes a claim feel understandable, and the presentation begins treating that feeling as verification. The audience may follow the analogy while the decisive factual step remains unsupported.

**How to use it:** For each important claim, ask what the evidence directly shows and what extra step your conclusion requires. Write the missing step in ordinary language. Decide whether to gather evidence, narrow the claim or present it explicitly as a proposal. Preserve the useful explanation while withdrawing any certainty it cannot support.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-evidence_gap).

#### Say what you expected before describing what happened

**Try this when:** A story announces a surprising outcome, but the audience never understood what the person expected or why. The surprise is then a fact without a useful change in interpretation.

**How to use it:** Choose an incident where your own prediction was wrong and the correction matters to the audience's decision. State the prediction and its reason before the result. Explain the changed understanding without adding reactions you did not observe or know. If the event is private, use a permitted account or clearly label a hypothetical example.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-expectation_reversal).

#### Translate the description into what it means for the listener

**Try this when:** An accurate technical explanation leaves the audience asking what difference it makes. More detail in the same style may not answer the practical question.

**How to use it:** After a technical statement, add a sentence beginning, for this decision, that means. Explain the relevant consequence and any exception. If you cannot explain the link, do not hide the gap behind a confident instruction. Match the urgency to the situation.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-register_shift).

#### Name who you are addressing when the request changes

**Try this when:** A speaker moves from one person or group to another without signaling the change. Responsibilities become blurred, or a broader audience is recruited to distract from an unanswered challenge.

**How to use it:** Before turning to another group, answer the current question or say when you will return to it. Then name the next group and what you need from them: ‘For the hosts, the next task is to confirm the room.’ If someone belongs to both groups, explain how the two requests fit together. Check that the change has not left a concern unanswered.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-audience_shift).

#### Share credit without denying your own contribution

**Try this when:** An award, introduction or personal story centers attention on the speaker. The closing needs to recognize the larger effort without sounding like ritual self-denial or immediately returning the spotlight to the speaker.

**How to use it:** When receiving recognition, acknowledge your actual contribution without inflating or erasing it. Identify the wider effort the occasion can make visible and end on a concrete feature of that effort. Do not claim to speak for everyone involved or use the recognition to promise what another group has not agreed to deliver.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-credit_redirection).

#### Say who should take the next step

**Try this when:** A presentation ends with a moving account of one person's work, but the next participants do not know what they can carry forward or how responsibility will pass to them.

**How to use it:** At the end of an update or proposal, distinguish people you are thanking from people you are asking to act. For each proposed transfer, state the next action, its resources and the acceptance step. Keep unresolved risks and support needs visible. A handoff is complete only when the receiving party has the information and authority it needs, not when the speaker finishes talking.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-hands_off).

#### Invite learners to correct what you think you know

**Try this when:** An account of leadership or mentoring makes one person the permanent source of wisdom. The people receiving help appear only as evidence of the leader's generosity.

**How to use it:** In a leadership story, identify a concrete instance when a junior colleague, participant or customer changed your understanding. Explain what you did differently afterward. Keep responsibility with the right person: being open to learning does not remove a manager's duties to the people they supervise.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-reciprocal_learning).

#### Show your experience without claiming to be always right

**Try this when:** An introduction needs to show why you are worth hearing, but a list of achievements can make it sound as if your status makes your advice always right. Empty self-deprecation creates the opposite problem: the audience cannot tell what you know how to do.

**How to use it:** State the experience that qualifies you to address the topic, then remove a specific implication that would go too far. For example, practical experience with one setting does not make you an authority on every setting. Let the limit sharpen the contribution you can make. Do not invent an exaggerated credential merely to manufacture a charming correction.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-self_undercut).

#### Name what is hard and what can still be done

**Try this when:** A description of helplessness leaves no room for a person's choices, but a sudden optimistic ending can erase the seriousness of what came before. The audience is asked to feel differently before being given a reason to reconsider.

**How to use it:** State what is difficult without exaggerating it for dramatic effect. Offer a concrete reason the initial interpretation may be incomplete: available help, a remaining option or a changed constraint. Preserve what that reason does not solve. If you have no basis for reassurance, honest acknowledgment may be more appropriate than manufacturing a hopeful turn.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-despair_to_hope).

#### Treat a milestone as progress, not completion

**Try this when:** A celebration turns a useful count into a finish line. After congratulating the team, you still need to explain what the number leaves unfinished without making the achievement seem meaningless.

**How to use it:** When closing a project phase or celebrating a target, name the unit and the completed work accurately. Then state the next need in its own terms. If the original milestone was genuinely the agreed endpoint, do not quietly convert celebration into an unapproved extension of people's commitments; present new work as a new decision.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-metric_revaluation).

#### Move from judging a decision to remembering a person

**Try this when:** A tribute follows a contested decision, and commemoration is mistaken for proof that the decision was right. Conversely, a remembrance can be overtaken by argument when the occasion has a different immediate purpose.

**How to use it:** Before writing a tribute, choose the contribution or relationship you can describe accurately. Check which memories you have permission to share. If there are unresolved questions about someone's decisions, explain where those questions will be addressed when that is relevant. Do not use the tribute to claim that everyone agrees or that further questions are unwelcome.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-moral_to_memorial).

#### Offer help without requiring people to take it

**Try this when:** A tool or technique that helps some people is presented as something everyone must use. The aid becomes a ritual, and the underlying task disappears behind compliance with the method.

**How to use it:** When introducing a template, checklist or rehearsal exercise, explain what problem it is intended to address. State what the person must accomplish and which other routes are acceptable. If the aid is actually mandatory for a necessary shared process, say that plainly and explain the reason rather than calling it optional.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-optional_support).

#### Explain the question before answering it

**Try this when:** A question seems impossible or contradictory because a key term carries a meaning different from the one your argument uses. Answering immediately may concede an assumption you do not share.

**How to use it:** Write the question and circle the word that could have two meanings. State both meanings, say which one you are answering and give the answer. Check whether the other meaning raises a question you still owe an answer to. Keep any agreed or formal definition clear; do not change it to make your claim easier to defend.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-question_redefinition).

#### Name what continues without erasing the loss

**Try this when:** A narrative includes loss and subsequent activity, but the transition makes the second seem to cancel the first. Continuing work is presented as if it demonstrates emotional resolution.

**How to use it:** Check the words linking a passage about loss to one about what continues. Words such as therefore, finally or now can claim more than you know. Choose words that show the real link: afterward, alongside, or simply say that both are true. Do not invent a tidy ending just to make the story feel complete.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-sorrow_and_continuation).

#### Explain what the story shows—and what it does not

**Try this when:** A presentation tells a vivid story and then announces a broad lesson. The audience may remember the event without seeing why that lesson follows, or may mistake a single case for proof of a general rule.

**How to use it:** After writing the story, add a sentence that says what changed in your understanding. Then write the main point and ask whether the story only illustrates it or gives enough evidence to support it. If you need broader evidence, add it or narrow the point. Do not tack on a slogan just to end the story.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-story_to_thesis).

#### Tell listeners what to pay attention to

**Try this when:** An opening, demonstration or audience activity is designed to attract attention, but the presentation never specifies what the audience should notice or understand as a result.

**How to use it:** Before adding a surprising image, question or activity, write what it should help the audience distinguish. Choose details that serve that purpose and remove those that compete with it. After the example, state the intended connection. #### Thank people by naming what they did

**Try this when:** An acknowledgment calls everyone amazing but gives the audience no account of what anyone did. The people doing less visible work may disappear behind the overall success.

**How to use it:** Replace a general thank-you with a sentence naming what the person did: checked the figures, arranged the room or explained a missing step. Add why that work mattered. Check the name and role, ask before sharing private details and make sure the wording does not give one person credit for everyone else's work.

#### Name what is unfinished and what you will do next

**Try this when:** A success story ends as though the problem has been solved permanently. When the issue returns in a new setting, the speaker either hides the recurrence or treats it as evidence that all earlier progress was false.

**How to use it:** Write what is working now and whether it would keep working if the people, workload or setting changed. Name the remaining task, who will do it and when they will check the result. Explain how this task builds on the earlier achievement. Keep the promise limited to work you can actually commit to.

#### Use a familiar example to explain, not to prove

**Try this when:** An abstract system feels remote. Listing recognizable places can make it tangible, but the audience may mistake recognition of the examples for evidence that the entire claim is true.

**How to use it:** Choose a few familiar situations that truly fit the idea you are explaining. Say what they have in common and what your claim does not cover. Use other evidence before saying the pattern is common or caused by something. Do not assume that recognizing an example means the audience agrees with your explanation.

#### Name what groups share without hiding their differences

**Try this when:** A presentation needs to connect people or activities at different scales, but unity language can imply that their needs, beliefs or responsibilities are identical.

**How to use it:** List what each group wants, what it can decide and what it needs from the others. Use those details to write one specific statement of what they share. Check that statement with the groups before speaking for them. Include a difference that still matters to the proposal.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-unity_across_scale).

#### Say what your role lets you do—and what it does not

**Try this when:** A speaker's title, fame or expertise creates attention, but the presentation never explains how that standing serves the task. Status may also be mistaken for competence outside the speaker's field.

**How to use it:** Name the resource your role gives you and the task it can responsibly support. Identify what still requires someone else's expertise or decision. If you introduce specialists, give them a real opportunity to speak rather than treating their presence as endorsement of everything you say. Keep the people served visible as participants with their own perspectives.

#### Name the skill or support a hard moment calls for

**Try this when:** A hard experience is presented as inherently character-building. The audience hears that difficulty is good for them without learning which skill, support or decision could actually help.

**How to use it:** Describe the task someone has to complete, then list the preparation, equipment or help it requires. Ask whether any unnecessary difficulty can be removed. For the speaking exercise, choose an ordinary task you can discuss without asking anyone to repeat dangerous activities described in a source.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-difficulty_to_capability).

#### Make the first step clear and limited

**Try this when:** People may be interested in a cause or activity but cannot tell what joining requires. A broad invitation to get involved hides the time, knowledge or commitment expected of them.

**How to use it:** Describe the first action separately from any ongoing role. List practical requirements and possible barriers, including time, location, equipment and social expectations. Offer a way to ask questions or decline. Do not use a small initial request to conceal a larger obligation that participants will discover only after accepting.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-invitation_to_low_barrier_entry).

#### Name the barrier before offering help

**Try this when:** A presentation praises generosity or promises access without explaining what prevents someone from taking the next step. The proposed help may sound admirable while missing the actual obstacle.

**How to use it:** For a service, product or community presentation, find the exact step where someone cannot continue. Check whether the problem is information, language, physical access, time, equipment or eligibility. Offer help for that problem, ask whether it is wanted and name later conditions you cannot change.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-access_obstacle_to_support).

#### Tell a personal story, then return to the shared task

**Try this when:** A personal account carries emotional weight, and you need to continue without pretending the feeling has disappeared or making the audience responsible for restoring your composure.

**How to use it:** Decide how much personal detail the talk needs. Write a simple way back to the subject and keep another version without the sensitive part. You can pause, use notes or change course; you do not need to share something private to prove you are being sincere. Protect other people's privacy as carefully as your own.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-emotion_to_composure).

#### Explain how these examples were chosen

**Try this when:** A presentation shows selected questions, enthusiastic participants or memorable behavior as though they speak for the whole audience. It leaves out how those examples were chosen and whether a reward or pressure affected what people said.

**How to use it:** When you share audience examples, say who supplied them, how you chose them and whether a reward or pressure may have mattered. Tell listeners whether an example was chosen to make a point or to estimate what a larger group thinks. To learn about quieter or absent participants, gather information that includes them.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-selection_and_incentive).

#### Ask whether the goal itself was wrong

**Try this when:** A disappointment leads immediately to trying harder or choosing a new route to the same target. The presentation never considers whether the original success standard was the problem.

**How to use it:** Write the target and the benefit it was meant to produce. Record whether each happened. If you met the target but missed the benefit, explain why the target may have been the wrong measure. Propose what you would check next time. Keep the useful work, costs and missed promises from the first attempt clear.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-failure_to_reframe).

#### Do not judge a whole person by their public style

**Try this when:** A polished speaker is described as fake, or an unpolished moment is treated as proof of sincerity. The presentation turns surface style into a judgment about someone's private identity.

**How to use it:** Try your draft aloud and decide where notes or rehearsal would help you say it accurately. If you describe another speaker, separate what you observed from what you guessed about their character. ‘She read from notes’ is an observation. It does not tell you whether she believes what she said.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-authenticity_vs_performance).

#### Show the steps between idea and finished work

**Try this when:** A finished piece looks inevitable. When you show only the result or describe a flash of inspiration, the audience has little to borrow for its own unfinished work.

**How to use it:** Choose one piece of work you have permission to discuss. Show its first usable draft, one important change and why you made it. Include something you dropped or a try that failed. End with a next step someone else can try; do not present your personal routine as the only way to be creative.

[See its made-up example, practice task and limits](#pattern-craft_process_over_outcome).

#### Answer the interruption, then continue the explanation

**Try this when:** You are halfway through an explanation when a listener objects. Continuing from the next slide may leave the misunderstanding intact; abandoning the entire explanation may lose the useful structure you already built.

**How to use it:** Pause long enough to identify the actual question. Restate it briefly when needed, answer the missing point and mark your return: with that distinction in place, the next step is. If you lack the answer, say what you cannot establish and whether the remaining explanation depends on it. Give unrelated questions a clear later route rather than pretending they were answered.

### Help the audience know where they are going

<a id="pattern-orientation_and_roadmap"></a>
#### Tell listeners where the talk is going, then follow that plan

**Main point:** Tell listeners which questions or parts you will cover. As you move through the talk, use the same names so they can follow the plan. At the end, show how the parts answer the opening question. Include only sections that help with the audience's task, and promise only what you have time to explain.

**When it may help:** Your material contains good points, but listeners cannot tell where they are, how the pieces relate or when you have answered the question you announced.

**How to try it:** Start from the audience's decision and choose the smallest set of sections needed to support it. Give each section a question, a contribution and a closing sentence. At the end, answer the announced questions in the same order. If the scope changes during discussion, say which section you are postponing instead of silently abandoning the promise.

**Source example — William McRaven: ten announced lessons, ten story units and a recap**

Summary of the linked transcript analysis.

**Invented practice example — Structuring a short internal tool proposal**

**Before:** I will cover the background, a few numbers, the options, some screenshots and anything else we have time for.

**After:** For this invented proposal: we need to decide whether to run a two-week trial. I will answer three questions: where does the current handoff fail, what would the trial change, and what would make us stop? First, the handoff. Later transitions repeat the second and third questions. The closing returns to them: we have a specific handoff to test, a limited change and a stop condition. The decision today is whether to authorize that trial—not whether to adopt the tool permanently.

**Why this change may help:** The roadmap makes each section accountable to the decision. The closing distinguishes the narrow authorization from a larger commitment. The script describes a presentation structure; it intentionally does not invent evidence that the hypothetical tool performs well.

**Ways to adapt it:**

1. For a technical audience, use the governing questions as section headings and keep detailed derivations beneath them. The audience should be able to recover the argument after inspecting a detail.
2. For a short pitch, two parts may suffice: the problem worth solving and the next test worth authorizing. A large numbered list can consume the time it is meant to organize.
3. For a discussion-led session, announce the decision order rather than a rigid speaking order. Name which question a contribution addresses before moving to the next.

**Try it:** Write your opening roadmap and closing recap on the same page. Draw a line from each promised question to the section that answers it and the sentence that summarizes the answer. Cut, move or label any material with no role in that route.

**Check:** Did every promised part arrive? Do the transition labels match the opening? Does the recap carry answers rather than just repeat topic names? Is the list short enough for your available time?

**Limit:** Example of what can go wrong: a presenter promises three lessons, introduces seven more ideas and ends with a motivational quote. The extra material may be interesting, but the audience has to reconstruct the promised route. Another failure is a beautifully completed roadmap whose sections never justify the requested decision.

### Make an unfamiliar idea easier to picture

<a id="pattern-definition_and_explanation"></a>
#### Define key words with a clear example

**Main point:** Say what a word means in this discussion. Give an example that fits and a similar example that does not. **How to try it:** Find a word that listeners must understand to judge your recommendation. Write what it means here, one example that fits and one similar example that does not. ### Answer hard questions without dodging them

<a id="pattern-question_and_reframe"></a>
#### Answer the decision behind the question

**Main point:** Check whether the question gives you enough room to answer accurately. If someone asks whether A or B comes first, the process may start with something else. Say what the question leaves out, explain the actual order and give an example. Then return to what the person wanted to know. Changing the question is useful only if it helps you answer their concern.

**When it may help:** A question gives you two options, but neither describes how the work actually happens. Picking one would sound decisive while giving the audience the wrong explanation.

**How to try it:** Write the question as it was asked. Under it, name the detail or option it leaves out. Start your answer by explaining why that detail matters, then answer the original concern. If someone asked for a decision, state your decision after the explanation.

**Before:** That is the wrong question. We need to think more strategically.

**After:** You are asking whether design or engineering should lead the first week. Before either team builds, we need to agree which customer task is failing. In this hypothetical project, users can find the report but cannot tell which version is current. It replaces a broad contest between disciplines with a dependency: agree on the problem, then test the relevant parts. **Ways to adapt it:**

1. For a time-limited Q&A, use one distinction and one answer. A long explanation of why the question is imperfect can consume the time needed to address it.
2. For an expert audience, state exactly which assumption you are changing. They may agree with the original framing for reasons you have not considered.

<a id="pattern-future_hope_and_action"></a>

<a id="pattern-metrics_and_revaluation"></a>

#### Name what is hard and what can still be done

**Main point:** After describing a difficulty, give a concrete reason that the situation may allow more than it first seemed: help is available, an option remains or a condition has changed. Explain what this new information leaves unresolved. Offer the audience a reason to reconsider without telling them how they should feel.

**When it may help:** A description of helplessness leaves no room for a person's choices, but a sudden optimistic ending can erase the seriousness of what came before. The audience is asked to feel differently before being given a reason to reconsider.

**How to try it:** State what is difficult without exaggerating it for dramatic effect. Offer a concrete reason the initial interpretation may be incomplete: available help, a remaining option or a changed constraint. Preserve what that reason does not solve. If you have no basis for reassurance, honest acknowledgment may be more appropriate than manufacturing a hopeful turn.

**Invented practice example — Addressing disappointment after a venue becomes unavailable**

**Before:** Losing the venue is a disaster, but everything happens for a reason and this will turn out better.

**After:** In this hypothetical update, losing the venue means the planned exhibition cannot open on that date. The preparation is not all lost: the object list, captions and contributor permissions remain useful. We have not secured a replacement space, so I cannot promise a new opening. We can now assess a smaller display or a later date using work already completed. The setback changes the plan without erasing every available option.

**Why this change may help:** The revised frame rests on identifiable work and options, not a claim that disappointment guarantees a better outcome. It names the unresolved venue problem plainly. The hopeful element is a narrower interpretation of what has been lost.

**Ways to adapt it:**

1. For a team update, distinguish remaining options from approved plans. Possibility can support discussion without being announced as a commitment.
2. For a personal address, offer your interpretation as your own. Other people may not share the same meaning or be ready to adopt it.
3. For a sensitive situation, avoid forced silver linings. Acknowledging difficulty and offering practical support can be sufficient without narrating a transformation.

**Try it:** Write the difficult fact, the interpretation it initially suggests and one evidence-based reason that interpretation may be incomplete. Add a sentence preserving what remains unresolved. Remove any prediction you cannot support.

**Check:** What justifies the hopeful turn? Does it change the interpretation without denying the facts? Are listeners free to respond differently, and is uncertainty still explicit?

**Limit:** Example of what can go wrong: a leader responds to lost resources by insisting that constraints are always blessings. The claim denies costs and discourages realistic planning. Another failure invents a reassuring outcome because the presentation seems to need an uplifting close.

## Put the pieces together: a complete presentation

### The request: approve one small test

This full example is invented for practice. It is not a real archive or proof that the proposed session would work. The speaker coordinates a small community archive. A committee can approve staff time and public events. The speaker asks it to approve one 90-minute session to describe photographs that have been cleared for use.

The invented facts are limited: volunteers scanned 120 photographs, and 45 records are missing a name, place or approximate date. Missing details do not always make a photograph unusable. A visitor's memory may also be uncertain. The speaker cannot promise that the session will identify every photograph, verify its history or grant permission to publish private information.

### The first draft

Our digitization project has been a huge success. We have preserved 120 photographs for everyone, but we need to take engagement to the next level. Community memory is our greatest resource, and a workshop will unlock it. We should invite everyone to bring their stories, identify all the missing information and make the archive truly accessible. It will be easy because our volunteers are passionate. Please support this exciting next step.

The draft sounds enthusiastic but leaves the decision unclear. Scanning is treated as if it guaranteed preservation and access. Attendance is treated as if it would identify every photograph. Volunteer enthusiasm is treated as a substitute for staff time. The committee cannot tell what it is approving, what it will cost or what the session is meant to find out. Stronger adjectives would not answer those questions.

### The revised presentation

We have scanned 120 photographs. That is real work worth recognizing: volunteers prepared the files, checked the image list and kept the records together. Today I am asking for a separate next step—one ninety-minute session to test whether we can add useful context to a small group of photographs.

Here is the distinction behind the request. Imagine opening a clear scan of a street gathering. You can see the people and the buildings, but the record does not tell you where or when it happened. A scan makes the image available to inspect; it does not supply the missing context. In our current set, 45 records lack at least one name, place or approximate date. That count describes incomplete fields, not 45 images with no value.

The proposed session would use twelve photographs cleared for this purpose. Participants could offer information, say they are uncertain or leave a photograph unidentified. We would record recollections with their uncertainty, not turn every confident answer into a confirmed fact. Questions about verification and permission would remain visible before any new information was published.

The concern about volunteer time is reasonable. I propose two hosts for the ninety-minute session: three volunteer hours between them. Preparation and review would take no more than four additional volunteer hours, making seven hours in total. If people cannot agree to take on that work, we postpone. We would use existing space and equipment, with no new purchases for this test.

We would review three things afterward: how many records gained potentially useful context, which claims still need checking and how much work the session required. Attendance alone will not tell us whether the process helped. We may learn that a different format is needed or that some photographs will remain unidentified.

So the decision today is limited: may we prepare this one session within that time allowance, subject to the material and staffing checks? Approval would not commit us to a recurring program. The scanning milestone remains an achievement. This next step asks what else a reader needs when the image alone cannot answer the question.

### A shorter version that keeps the decision clear

This shorter script uses the same invented facts: ‘Our volunteers scanned 120 photographs. Forty-five records still lack a name, place or approximate date. May we prepare one ninety-minute workshop using twelve photographs cleared for this purpose? Two hosts would give three hours between them, with up to four more volunteer hours for preparation and review. We would use existing space and equipment. If we cannot fill those roles, we postpone. Visitors’ suggestions would stay marked as uncertain until checked. Afterward, we would report what information was added, what still needs checking and how much work it took. Today’s request covers one session.’

This version leaves out the imagined street scene and the longer explanation of why scanning matters. It keeps the decision, the relevant numbers, the work required and the conditions for proceeding. To shorten your own talk, first protect the details a listener needs to decide. Then cut examples or explanation that this audience already understands.

### Why these choices belong together

The opening credits the volunteers and gives the committee the request at once. It treats 120 scans as completed work, not proof that the archive is ready for every use.

The imagined street scene helps the audience picture why missing details matter. It is clearly made up; it does not claim that a real visitor reacted this way. The number that follows gives a separate fact about the records. The example explains the problem; the count shows its size in this invented case.

The plan is limited: show a cleared set of photographs, collect possible details, mark what is uncertain and review the notes. A meeting cannot turn a memory into verified history. The speaker also answers the concern about staff time by making the proposal smaller and allowing it to be postponed if no one can do the work.

The ending repeats the difference between scanning and adding context, then asks the committee to approve one session. It does not add a new claim at the last moment. Several speaking choices support the same request, but the presentation does not name those techniques.

### Prepare for the question that could change the decision

These exchanges continue the invented workshop example. Question: ‘Will this fill the gaps in all 45 records?’ Answer: ‘No. We propose using twelve photographs, and some may remain unidentified. The session would help us find out what information visitors can offer and what work it takes to check it.’ The answer starts with the limit, then explains what the committee could learn from the session.

Question: ‘Who will check whether a visitor’s memory is accurate?’ Answer: ‘We need to assign that work before the session. The four additional hours cover preparation and review together; they may not be enough to settle every question. Any detail we cannot check stays marked as uncertain. I cannot promise that every suggestion will be ready to publish.’ This answer keeps unfinished work visible instead of inventing a person or process that the plan does not yet have.

Question: ‘Why don’t we approve a monthly workshop now?’ Answer: ‘We don’t yet know whether this format gives us useful information at a workload we can sustain. I’m asking for one session. After we review its results and volunteer time, we can bring you a separate proposal for more.’ This keeps the original request clear when someone proposes a larger commitment.

Question: ‘Is the time request four hours or seven?’ Answer: ‘Seven volunteer hours in total: two hosts for ninety minutes each, plus up to four hours shared across preparation and review. We would postpone if that work cannot be assigned.’ State both parts whenever a total could otherwise be mistaken for only one part of the work.

A real version would need to follow the archive's policies, permissions, staffing and fact-checking process. This example is only a speaking exercise, not advice on running an archive or handling legal questions. Replace every invented detail with checked local facts before using a version of it.

### Practise the argument, then revise the wording

Read the script aloud and time your own delivery rather than treating a word count as a guaranteed duration. Remove a sentence if it repeats a job already done; retain it if removing it hides a condition the decision needs. Practise a shorter version that keeps the request, the scan/context distinction, the resource limit and the review question.

For a self-check, close the script and write down the exact decision requested, the strongest claim supported by the facts and the main uncertainty. Compare your answers with the script. If another reader is available, ask the same questions without giving them hints. Their response is feedback on this draft, not proof that the approach works for everyone.

Now use your own presentation. Write the decision first, mark every factual claim and identify the evidence it needs. Choose one concrete example, one important limitation and one genuine objection. Draft the ending before polishing the opening. The useful result is a presentation another person can inspect and respond to, not one that merely sounds finished.

## Your next draft

### Keep the point visible

A useful presentation makes its example, main difference, limits and request clear. Which of those matters most depends on your subject, evidence and audience.

As you revise, ask what may confuse the listener. Add context to an example, a unit to a number, an answer to an objection or a clear request at the end. Fix that before polishing individual phrases.

Remove claims the facts do not support. Keep uncertainty where it matters. Use stories to explain, not to claim more than they show. Ask for a response without assuming the answer will be yes. The goal is to help your audience follow your reasoning and decide what they think.

### Opening a talk: get attention, then give it a direction

In its public-speaking lesson, Charisma on Command says a speech should catch attention quickly, then discusses connecting the subject to the audience. A later section uses a personal story to make that point. [Watch ‘How to Get Better at Public Speaking Fast’ around 1:54–2:01 and 3:46–3:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zUBDMZNayj4&t=114s).

This book adds a practical check: after the opening, can listeners tell what the talk is about and where it is going? A joke or story may help earn attention, but it should lead into the question, choice or idea the talk will address. Attention by itself does not show that people understood.

Made-up practice example, using the archive workshop from the earlier chapter. Before: ‘Welcome to an exciting new chapter for our archive. Today I want to talk about the power of community memory.’ The words name a large subject, but the committee still has to guess what decision is coming.

After: ‘We scanned 120 photographs. Forty-five records are still missing a name, place or approximate date. Today I’m asking you to approve one 90-minute workshop where visitors can help us investigate those gaps. I’ll explain what we need, how we’ll record uncertain answers and what we’ll check afterward.’

The missing details give the audience a reason to listen. The request and outline tell them what to listen for. To try this in your own opening, name one specific problem, the decision you need and the questions your talk will answer.

### Starting a deeper conversation: invite, do not pressure

In a video about deeper conversations, the presenter describes moving from ordinary topics toward feelings, motives or values. He uses a question about how an experience felt as an example. [Watch ‘How to Have Deeper Conversations With Anyone’ around 2:03–2:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BfUEDvrD1Y4&t=123s).

That may suit a willing one-to-one conversation. In a presentation or work meeting, a personal question can put someone on the spot. The related chapter in this book uses a safer test: make the response clear, relevant to the subject and easy to decline.

Made-up practice example. Before: ‘Go around the room and tell us a family memory that shows why this archive matters.’ Some people may have a story they want to share. Others may be new to the area, prefer privacy or simply want to discuss the plan.

After: ‘What would you need to know before helping at the workshop: what the task involves, how much time it takes or how we handle uncertain answers? You can answer now, send a question afterward or pass.’

The new question invites a useful response without asking for a personal history. The alternatives give people something specific to consider while leaving room for another concern. For your own meeting, ask about the decision or task first; leave personal stories to people who choose to offer them.

### Repairing an awkward moment: be direct, but check safety

In a video about awkward interviews, Charisma on Command suggests reading an unclear comment generously in one example, naming discomfort in another, and asking a direct question about whether the interviewer should continue a topic. [Watch ‘What to Say When a Conversation Gets Awkward’ around 1:38–2:07 and 2:48–2:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_2tXNCxg8hs&t=98s).

The useful choice is to address the moment instead of answering with another dig. But ‘assume good intent’ is not safe advice for every situation. If someone is threatening, harassing or using their power against you, protect yourself and set a clear limit; you do not owe them a friendly interpretation.

Made-up practice example. A committee member says, ‘Is this another workshop that creates work for everyone else?’ Before: ‘That’s unfair. Our volunteers work hard.’ This defends the volunteers but leaves the concern about workload unanswered.

After: ‘Are you concerned about staffing the session or checking the information afterward?’ Suppose the member answers, ‘The checking afterward.’ You could reply: ‘That work belongs in the plan. I haven’t assigned it yet. Before asking you to approve the session, I need to name who will check the notes and how much time they can give.’

The question helps identify the concern; the reply then admits a missing part of the plan. Use this sequence when a comment is unclear: ask what the person means, hear the answer and respond to that specific concern. If a comment crosses a personal line, say so directly: ‘Please keep the comments about the proposal.’

### Showing respect: use evidence, not a display of importance

A Charisma on Command video about respect contrasts trying to prove one's worth with a calmer example. The presenter makes this point while discussing fictional characters, not a measured study of real audiences. [Watch ‘Trying to Get Respect Doesn’t Work, This Does’ around 8:25–9:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tg_yavuXU6g&t=505s).

For a presentation, a useful adaptation is to replace claims such as ‘I’m the expert’ with the facts listeners need: what you did, what changed, what remains uncertain and who contributed. This is a drafting suggestion—not a proven way to earn respect.

Made-up practice example. Before: ‘As the person who understands this collection best, I can tell you the workshop will succeed.’ The speaker asks the committee to accept a prediction because of their position.

After: ‘I coordinate the archive, and our volunteers completed the scans. The records show 45 photographs with missing details. A workshop could help us collect possible names, places and dates. We’ll keep those suggestions marked as uncertain until they are checked. I can explain the work involved; I cannot promise how many photographs visitors will identify.’

The speaker states their role, credits the volunteers and explains the proposal using information the committee can examine. For your own draft, replace one claim about your authority with the result or observation behind your recommendation. Keep any experience that helps listeners judge what you know.
