# Draft lesson: qualify predictions and interpretations precisely

Status: draft practice hypothesis; not learner-validated.

## Communication job

Tell the listener what kind of claim you are making, how certain it is, and
what evidence or future information could change it—without burying the main
point in vague language.

## Practice task

Write one claim, then label it as an observation, prediction, interpretation or
recommendation. Record four moves:

1. State the claim plainly.
2. Add the smallest qualifier that matches its uncertainty.
3. Say what the claim is based on.
4. Name what would change or narrow it.

Template:

> “I observed **[what is directly available]**. I interpret or predict **[claim]**
> with **[degree and kind of uncertainty]**, based on **[reason/evidence]**.
> I would revise this if **[condition]**.”

For a recommendation, separate “the evidence suggests” from “I recommend.”
For an unknown, say what is unknown rather than using “maybe” as decoration.

## Self-check

- Is the main claim still understandable?
- Does the qualifier attach to the correct sentence?
- Did I distinguish observation from interpretation, prediction or advice?
- Did I avoid both false certainty and empty vagueness?
- Did I name a revision condition or missing evidence?
- Does the uncertainty change the action the listener should take?

## When not to use it

Do not hedge a fact merely to avoid accountability, and do not state a fragile
interpretation as settled truth. Do not use a vague “I think” when a precise
source, confidence range, condition or limitation would be clearer. Provide
written definitions, captions or extra processing time when the distinction is
complex; vocal tentativeness is not the goal.

## Learner check

Give the learner five statements and ask them to classify each as observation,
prediction, interpretation or recommendation, then attach an appropriate limit.
Ask them to rewrite one overconfident claim and one over-hedged claim. A second
listener should identify the claim type and the condition that would change it.

This tests calibration and transfer. It does not establish that hedging improves
trust, memory or action.
