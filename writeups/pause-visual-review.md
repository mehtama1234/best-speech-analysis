## Three different evidence situations

| Source and requested seeks | Measured quiet interval at −40 dBFS | Direct still-frame evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [White House backdrop](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2fWJh-_UG5s&t=92), 92.8 / 93.8 / 94.9 s | 93.12–94.62 s | Recognizable scene persists at sampled instants; head/hand configurations change; subtitle appears in final still. |
| [Handheld microphone](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fBnAMUkNM2k&t=196), 196.6 / 197.4 / 198.2 s | 196.94–197.88 s | Recognizable shot persists at sampled instants; bottom text disappears, then a new clause appears. |
| [Film dialogue](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gDadfh0ZdBM&t=131), 131.9 / 132.8 / 133.7 s | 132.24–133.50 s | First still shows one seated character; second and third show another character in a different shot. |

The third case supplies a concrete editing boundary: one numerical quiet
interval is near a visible change of shot, not simply an isolated image of a
person waiting to speak. The shot-change bracket overlaps the quiet interval
**only if the separate audio and video timelines align**. The exact cut could
be before or within that interval; no synchronization has yet been verified.
Do not call it a reaction shot based on presumed emotion, assign a speaker turn
from these images, or claim the edit caused the quiet audio.

The first two cases do not establish the opposite. Matching sampled scenes
cannot rule out cuts between them. A changed hand configuration is not a
measured gesture trajectory. A brow or mouth configuration does not establish
an emotion. A subtitle disappearing is an editorial observation, not validated
word timing or evidence of deliberate suspense.

## What this changes in the research

Quiet audio, caption gaps, visible movement and rhetorical pauses must remain
separate fields. In particular, the film example already contradicts an older
ten-second caption-gap rationale; these stills add an editing confound rather
than rescuing that rationale. See the [pause evidence boundaries](pause-evidence-boundaries.md).

No legacy pause decision is promoted. All seven still require semantic
adjudication; this visual pass covers three of them. The sample establishes
neither prevalence nor a communication benefit. Five provisional language
practice cards and six exploratory cross-passage comparisons remain unchanged.

Next, validate audio/video alignment and decoded timestamps, listen to and
watch continuous local clips, identify turns and shot/subtitle boundaries, and
compare with non-quiet windows. Only then test whether a pause marks an idea,
turn transition, reading interval or edit. Independent review and broader
sampling are still required for recurring-pattern claims.
