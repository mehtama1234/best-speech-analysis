# Ellen DeGeneres “Be True To Yourself”: use comic permission, tell the cost of a value, and let the closing principle grow out of the story

The transcript appears to be a 2009 commencement address to a graduating class.

## What the speech does, in order

### 1. It earns attention by making the formal room less formal

The opening thanks the president and guests, then teases a “creepy Spanish
teacher,” says the graduates may be hungover and tells them they cannot graduate
until the speech is over (`bDth6cv5raI:00000`–`bDth6cv5raI:00005`). The audience is
not treated as an abstract crowd. The speaker gives them a shared morning,
headache and deadline.

The pattern is comic permission: a formal speaker names the awkward reality in
the room, lowers distance and earns attention before offering a lesson. The
limits are important. Humor about alcohol, teachers or a person’s body may land
differently across audiences, and a speaker without permission can make a room
less safe. A product should teach learners to diagnose the room, not copy the
jokes.

### 2. It turns a word into a running image

The speaker says she looked up “commencement,” then invents a breakdown into
“common” and “cement.” Cement becomes sidewalks, sidewalks have cracks and the
old superstition about breaking one’s mother’s back follows
(`bDth6cv5raI:00005`–`bDth6cv5raI:00013`). She then calls the ceremony “common
cement.”

This is not a true word history; it is playful invention. Its speech function is
to make the event’s name memorable and give the talk a recurring image. A useful
pattern can be built from a false or literal reading only if the speaker signals
the joke clearly. Learners should not present wordplay as fact.

### 3. It lowers status distance before claiming relevance

She says she did not attend the college, has no college degree and jokes that she
graduated from the “school of hard knocks” (`bDth6cv5raI:00014`–
`bDth6cv5raI:00022`). The joke lets her say, “I am not one of you,” without
making that difference an apology. She then answers why she is there: because of
the class, because she loves New Orleans and because she grew up there
(`bDth6cv5raI:00023`–`bDth6cv5raI:00031`).

The move is a relevance bridge: acknowledge the credential gap, identify the
shared place or purpose, and explain why the speaker still has something useful
to say. It does not mean formal education is unimportant, or that hardship is a
substitute for knowledge. The product lesson is to state the basis of relevance
honestly rather than pretend to share an experience one does not have.

### 4. It uses a messy work history to make uncertainty normal

She says that after school she was lost, had no ambition and did not know what
she wanted. She lists jobs—shucking oysters, hostess, bartender, waitress,
painting houses and selling vacuum cleaners—and describes a modest goal of paying
rent and maybe basic cable (`bDth6cv5raI:00032`–`bDth6cv5raI:00039`).

The list does two jobs. It gives the audience concrete scenes instead of a
general “I struggled,” and it removes the false idea that a meaningful life
requires a clear plan at graduation. The list is funny because it is specific,
but the underlying point is serious. A safe adaptation says uncertainty is
common without promising that a lucky career path will appear.

### 5. It pivots from comedy to a serious loss

The speaker says she thought she knew herself at the graduates’ age, then tells
of a girlfriend killed in a car accident. She passed the accident without knowing
who it was and learned shortly afterward (`bDth6cv5raI:00040`–
`bDth6cv5raI:00050`). She pairs the loss with the harsh details of a basement
apartment, no heat or air, a mattress on the floor and fleas.

The abrupt pivot changes the audience’s reading of the earlier jokes. The messy
life is not only a comic résumé; it is the setting in which the speaker searched
for meaning. The physical details make poverty and grief concrete, but they must
not be treated as required ingredients of insight. A product should teach a
choice point: a speaker can tell a serious story, signal the transition, and
stop before details become exposure or spectacle.

### 6. It turns an unanswerable question into creative work

She asks why the death happened, wonders why fleas are in the apartment and jokes
that it would be convenient to call God for answers (`bDth6cv5raI:00050`–
`bDth6cv5raI:00055`). Instead, she writes an imaginary one-sided conversation
with God. She then imagines performing it on *The Tonight Show* and describes
the later outcome (`bDth6cv5raI:00055`–`bDth6cv5raI:00063`).

The reusable move is not “turn grief into success.” It is: when an answer is not
available, make a form that can hold the question. Writing, drawing, prayer,
conversation or a private ritual may help some people; none is a guaranteed
response to bereavement. The story also shows a useful future image: a specific
stage and act, not a vague dream.

### 7. It names the hidden cost of public success

She says stand-up and a sitcom became successful, but she was trying to please
everyone while keeping her sexuality secret (`bDth6cv5raI:00063`–
`bDth6cv5raI:00070`). She describes shame and fear, then says she chose to come
out and make it creative by having the character come out too
(`bDth6cv5raI:00071`–`bDth6cv5raI:00076`).

The pattern is success on the outside → hidden cost → value-based decision. It
makes “be yourself” more credible because the speaker names what self-concealment
was costing her. It also requires a strong boundary: disclosure is not morally
required, and safety, housing, employment, family and legal conditions differ.
The product must teach authenticity as choosing what is safe and true for the
speaker, not compulsory public disclosure.

### 8. It states the feared consequence before showing it happened

She asks what the worst outcome could be, answers that she could lose her career,
and then says plainly, “I did.” The show was cancelled, she read it in the paper,
the phone did not ring for three years and no one wanted to touch her
(`bDth6cv5raI:00077`–`bDth6cv5raI:00084`).

This is an especially strong truth pattern: name the risk, pause the audience’s
hope, confirm the cost. It prevents the story from becoming a dishonest success
myth. It also creates a safety lesson: a speaker should not promise that honesty
will be rewarded. The right question is what support, resources and options exist
if the feared cost occurs.

### 9. It holds harm, purpose and recovery in one passage

She says letters from young people who nearly died by suicide made her realize
the action had a purpose (`bDth6cv5raI:00085`–`bDth6cv5raI:00089`). She also says
she felt punished, angry and sad. A talk-show offer arrives, stations resist it,
and she later says she would not change the experience because losing everything
helped her find what mattered (`bDth6cv5raI:00089`–`bDth6cv5raI:00098`).

The speech refuses a smooth recovery arc: purpose does not cancel anger, and a
later opportunity does not erase the loss. The risk is retrospective meaning
being heard as advice to endure harm because it may help others. The product
lesson should require two separate sentences: what was harmful, and what meaning
the speaker later chose. Never use another person’s suicidal crisis as proof of a
career or disclosure strategy; any real safety concern needs appropriate crisis
support, not speech practice.

### 10. It changes the definition of success

The speaker begins with the old dream—fame, movies, cars and groupies—then says
her definition changed. She names integrity, resisting peer pressure, honesty,
compassion and contribution (`bDth6cv5raI:00099`–`bDth6cv5raI:000113`).

This is a value conversion: an external score becomes a way of living. The
change is persuasive because the story has already shown the cost of the old
score. The list is also measurable enough to practice: an honest choice, a
compassionate act or a contribution. It should not become a new purity test;
people can care about money, recognition and integrity at the same time.

### 11. It uses a repeated conclusion as a comic structure

She says “to conclude,” then “to conclude my conclusion,” and continues with
advice: follow passion, stay true, do not follow another path unless lost in the
woods, do not give advice and do not take advice (`bDth6cv5raI:00113`–
`bDth6cv5raI:00118`). The repeated ending makes the audience expect a stop, then
gives one more turn.

This is a structural joke, not confusion. It lets the speaker be serious without
pretending to deliver a perfect formula. “Do not take advice” is itself advice;
the contradiction invites the audience to use judgment. A product can teach the
form but should ask learners to signal when a contradiction is deliberate.

### 12. It offers reassurance, then undercuts it with time and place

The speaker says the graduates need not worry because the economy is booming,
the job market is open and the planet is fine (`bDth6cv5raI:00119`–
`bDth6cv5raI:00124`). These statements are comic reassurance in a 2009 setting,
not reliable forecasts. She then returns to a serious idea: devastating events
can teach important questions (`bDth6cv5raI:00125`–`bDth6cv5raI:00129`).

The pattern is reassurance → obvious overstatement → joke → useful question.
The limit is crucial: current facts change, and a joke about the economy,
employment or the planet should not be copied as information. A modern learner
should replace the false certainty with an honest uncertainty and a practical
next question.

### 13. It closes with a local image and a short memory line

The speech returns to “common cement,” calls life one big Mardi Gras and contrasts
showing one’s body with showing one’s brain (`bDth6cv5raI:00129`–
`bDth6cv5raI:00134`). It ends by telling the “Katrina class” that they will be
okay and asks them to remember a small phrase: just dance
(`bDth6cv5raI:00134`–`bDth6cv5raI:00135`).

The close uses local identity, a callback, reassurance and a compact action image.
The line works because the speech has already earned its seriousness; the dance
is not a denial of difficulty but a way to leave the room moving. The product
lesson is to build a close from earlier images and make it memorable without
promising that everything will be fine.

## Reusable pattern cards

### Pattern A — Comic permission before serious content

**Shape:** name the room’s awkward reality → make a light observation → earn
attention → state the real purpose.

**Example:** hungover graduates and a formal ceremony lead into the speech
(`bDth6cv5raI:00000`–`bDth6cv5raI:00005`).

**Try it when:** a formal audience is tense or distant.

**Do not use it to:** target vulnerable people, excuse prejudice or make the room
laugh before a story that needs care.

**Practice test:** ask one listener whether the joke opened attention or made them
feel exposed. Keep the purpose clear within the next two sentences.

### Pattern B — Relevance bridge without false credentials

**Shape:** name the difference in status or experience → identify the actual
connection → explain what the speaker can offer.

**Example:** no college attendance becomes New Orleans roots and a reason to be
there (`bDth6cv5raI:00014`–`bDth6cv5raI:00031`).

**Try it when:** speaking to an institution where you are not an alumnus or
insider.

**Do not use it to:** romanticize hardship or pretend credentials do not matter.

**Practice test:** write the basis of relevance in one factual sentence and one
sentence about the audience’s needs.

### Pattern C — Specific uncertainty list

**Shape:** admit not knowing → give several concrete attempts or jobs → show the
next decision without claiming a master plan.

**Example:** the list of jobs and modest rent goal
(`bDth6cv5raI:00032`–`bDth6cv5raI:00039`).

**Try it when:** an audience believes everyone should have a fixed path.

**Do not use it to:** imply uncertainty always leads to success.

**Practice test:** list three real experiments, what each taught and what remains
unknown.

### Pattern D — Name the cost of the value

**Shape:** value or decision → feared consequence → actual cost → support or
meaning that followed.

**Example:** honesty about sexuality, career loss, silence and later purpose
(`bDth6cv5raI:00063`–`bDth6cv5raI:00098`).

**Try it when:** “be yourself” would otherwise sound cheap.

**Do not use it to:** pressure disclosure or turn danger into proof of courage.

**Practice test:** write the value, risk, available support and safe alternative;
do not promise the cost will be worth it.

### Pattern E — Separate harm from later meaning

**Shape:** say what hurt → acknowledge what did not fix it → describe the meaning
chosen later → leave room for another person’s meaning.

**Example:** anger and career punishment sit beside purpose and recovery
(`bDth6cv5raI:00085`–`bDth6cv5raI:00098`).

**Try it when:** telling a recovery story.

**Do not use it to:** glorify suffering, use suicide references for inspiration or
tell people to remain in harm.

**Practice test:** have an independent listener identify the harm sentence and
the meaning sentence; neither should erase the other.

### Pattern F — Callback close with one small action

**Shape:** return to an early image → change its meaning → offer a short action or
phrase the audience can remember.

**Example:** common cement and Mardi Gras return before “just dance”
(`bDth6cv5raI:00129`–`bDth6cv5raI:00135`).

**Try it when:** a long talk needs a memorable exit.

**Do not use it to:** replace a needed plan with a slogan or promise safety.

**Practice test:** remove the final line and see whether the earlier image makes
the line understandable; if not, build the callback earlier.

## Product and learning implications

This source supports a “story-to-principle” lesson in the speech pattern lab.
The learner should build a commencement-style talk from a low-risk personal or
fictional story, identify the value it changed, name the cost without promising
reward, and end with a callback. The tool must explicitly allow the learner to
choose not to share personal identity, grief or trauma.

The practice flow should require:

- audience setting and a safe opening joke or non-comic alternative;
- honest basis of relevance;
- three concrete uncertainty or experiment details;
- story, value, risk, support and meaning kept in separate fields;
- a disclosure-safety check and private mode;
- success defined as behavior or values, not only status;
- current facts checked rather than borrowed from an old joke;
- an earlier image returned to in the close;
- a crisis stop and support route when the draft mentions suicide, abuse or
  immediate danger.

Feedback should score audience safety, humor fit, story clarity, cost honesty,
disclosure control, value clarity, factual accuracy, callback strength and action
specificity separately from charm or applause. It should flag “everything will be
fine,” suffering-as-proof, forced authenticity and jokes that target a person or
group.
