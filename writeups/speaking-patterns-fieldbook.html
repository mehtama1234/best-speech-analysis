How to use the fieldbook

Start with the question your audience needs to answer

Start by writing the question your audience should be able to answer after your talk. Perhaps they need to understand a problem, compare options or make a decision. That question will help you choose what to include—and what to leave out.

Here is a made-up example. You are asking a committee to approve a public workshop. A topic such as ‘our community project’ leaves you with too much to cover. A question gives you a useful limit: ‘Should we approve one workshop next month?’ Now you know you need to explain its purpose, the staff time it requires and what the committee would be agreeing to.

That question also helps you edit. A story about a visitor may explain why the workshop is needed. A list of every event you have held may add little to this decision. For each section, ask: ‘What does this help the committee decide?’ Keep the details needed to answer that question.

Choose the chapter that fits a problem in your draft. Read the example and the note about what it cannot show. Try the exercise in your own words and use facts you can support. The index helps you find related ideas; you do not need to use every one.

Do not make a claim bigger than its evidence. One story shows what happened in one account, not how often it happens. A comparison can show one similarity, not prove that two things work the same in every way. A request shows what the speaker asked for, not whether anyone agreed.

Speaking job

Help the audience know where they are going

Ask: What does the audience need to understand first, and what will you cover next?

Main point

Tell listeners what the talk is about, why it matters and what you will cover. Then cover what you promised. If the plan changes, say so. End by answering the opening question or stating what remains unknown.

A way to do it

  1. Write the audience’s main question in one sentence.
  2. Name the result, decision or explanation the talk can actually provide.
  3. Preview only the sections you have time to deliver; signal a change of direction when it happens.
  4. Close by answering the opening question or naming what remains unresolved.

Speaking job

Make an unfamiliar idea easier to picture

Ask: What does the audience need to picture, compare or understand?

Main point

Explain the idea without making it seem simpler or more certain than it is. Define the term, give a specific example and say how it helps. If you use a comparison, state what is similar and where the comparison stops.

A way to do it

  1. Name the idea in ordinary words.
  2. Give one example the audience can inspect.
  3. Say which part of the example illustrates the idea.
  4. State what the example cannot establish; add a second example if one case could mislead.

Speaking job

Use a story to show what changed

Ask: What did someone expect, what happened, and what does that change mean now?

Main point

Use a story when it helps explain a choice, result or change in belief. Say what the person expected, what happened and what they learned. Keep the lesson within what this one account can show: it cannot tell us how often the same thing happens or what everyone should do.

A way to do it

  1. Set the scene and the original expectation.
  2. Show the choice or event that interrupted it.
  3. Explain what changed—and what stayed true.
  4. Limit the lesson to this case unless other evidence supports a broader claim.

Speaking job

Answer hard questions without dodging them

Ask: What does the question assume, and what does the person asking need to decide?

Main point

Start by showing that you heard the question. Answer it directly when the facts allow. If the question leaves out a difference that matters, name it and explain why. Correct your own wording if it was too broad. Do not use a different question to avoid the one you were asked.

A way to do it

  1. Restate the concern briefly and fairly.
  2. Give the clearest supported answer first.
  3. Name any condition, missing fact or distinction that changes the answer.
  4. If you overstated something, correct the exact phrase and continue.

Speaking job

Show who did the work and who owns the next step

Ask: Who contributed, who can decide, and what support or responsibility remains?

Main point

Credit and responsibility are related but different. Name the work people actually did. Say who can make the next decision and what help they need. A personal story is not a substitute for a service, policy or named person responsible for the work. If no one measured the result, say so.

A way to do it

  1. Name specific contributions rather than praising an unnamed group.
  2. Separate what is already done from what is being requested.
  3. Say who decides, what help they need and who takes over any remaining work.
  4. Keep reported results separate from verified results.

Speaking job

Build connection without putting people on the spot

Ask: What relationship do your words create, and does the audience have a real choice?

Main point

A story, joke, identity or personal disclosure can help people see why a subject matters. It can also expose someone, reduce a group to a stereotype or pressure listeners to agree. Share only what is yours to share. Make your point without asking listeners to reveal private experiences or show a particular emotion.

A way to do it

  1. State the point in words; do not make a gesture, identity or joke carry it alone.
  2. Use a personal example only with a clear reason and a fair boundary.
  3. Make participation optional and offer a way to pass or respond privately.
  4. Check who bears the risk if the joke or disclosure is misunderstood.

Speaking job

Ask for a next step people can actually take

Ask: What action is possible for this audience, with the time, authority and support they have?

Main point

A useful request tells people what to do, who is responsible, what support is available and what happens if the condition is not met. Match the request to the audience’s actual role. Leave room to decline, ask for more information or choose another path. A strong closing can clarify a decision; it cannot prove that the decision was made or that the proposal worked.

A way to do it

  1. Name one action, not a cloud of good intentions.
  2. Identify the person or group that can take it and the resources required.
  3. State the time limit, condition, opt-out or alternative path.
  4. Say how you will check what happened without treating attendance or applause as success.

22 broad themes

These are the 22 main themes in the larger pattern writeup. Each link opens its related detailed entry in the 55-pattern index. The counts mean a transcript search found a match in that many videos; the matches have not all been checked.

1. Start with context before details

In simple terms: Set the scene → say why it matters → show where you are going.

Search matches: 147 videos.

Use this at the beginning of a presentation, section or answer. Tell people where they are, why the point matters, and how you will get there. Do not open with background that has no visible purpose.

Related detailed pattern: Tell listeners where the talk is going, then follow that plan

2. Use one specific story to explain an idea

In simple terms: Tell one specific moment → explain what it shows → say what it cannot prove.

Search matches: 170 videos.

Use one specific moment instead of a pile of abstractions. After the story, state the lesson explicitly; then prevent the anecdote from pretending to be general evidence.

Related detailed pattern: Use one scene to explain your point

3. Show what changed

In simple terms: Say what you thought → what changed → what you think now → what still holds.

Search matches: 167 videos.

Use this for before/after, disagreement, learning or a change of plan. Show the earlier position fairly. Contrast should clarify a difference, not create a false enemy.

Related detailed pattern: Explain what changed and what stayed the same

4. Use an analogy carefully

In simple terms: Give a comparison → say what matches → say where it stops matching.

Search matches: 167 videos.

Use an analogy to make an unfamiliar idea inspectable. Explain exactly which relationship transfers and where the comparison breaks.

Related detailed pattern: Return to an earlier image to add meaning

5. Turn hope into a specific next step

In simple terms: Say what you hope for → name one doable step → say what help or limits matter.

Search matches: 189 videos.

Use this near the end of a persuasive talk. Replace vague inspiration with one action, an owner, available support and a realistic boundary.

Related detailed pattern: Give hope a next action, not a guaranteed ending

6. Invite a clear response without pressure

In simple terms: Ask for a clear response → make it easy to answer → make it okay to decline.

Search matches: 196 videos.

Use it for questions, discussion, audience participation or a call to action. Make the requested response concrete and do not use participation pressure as a substitute for clarity.

Related detailed pattern: Return the question to the people who can act on it

7. Connect one person's experience to the bigger picture

In simple terms: Start with one person's experience → explain the larger rule or system → connect them.

Search matches: 165 videos.

Use this when a talk needs to connect an individual story to policy, culture or shared responsibility. Say when you are changing scale so the audience can follow the reasoning.

Related detailed pattern: Connect the total to one person's experience

8. State your point and its limits

In simple terms: Make your point → say when it applies and where it does not.

Search matches: 163 videos.

Use this when certainty is limited or the audience may overgeneralize. Put the main point in plain language, then state what would change it or where it does not apply.

Related detailed pattern: State your point and its limits

9. Name everyone's contribution

In simple terms: Name the work people did → say what it led to → name the support behind it.

Search matches: 158 videos.

Use this when acknowledging a team, community or predecessor. Name specific contributions without pretending that praise itself proves the result.

Related detailed pattern: Explain who helped produce the result

10. Say who is responsible for what

In simple terms: Name who decides → what help is available → who is still responsible.

Search matches: 154 videos.

Use it when a talk asks someone to act. Distinguish what the speaker can do, what another actor controls, and what help is needed.

Related detailed pattern: Name what people can actually choose

11. Choose a measure that reflects what matters

In simple terms: Name the usual measure → what it misses → a measure that better fits the decision.

Search matches: 153 videos.

Use it when numbers are dominating the conversation. Keep the useful part of the metric, expose its blind spot, and explain the better decision criterion.

Related detailed pattern: Choose a measure that answers the actual question

12. Answer the question behind the question

In simple terms: Repeat the question → explain the decision underneath it → answer with any needed condition.

Search matches: 149 videos.

Use it for interviews, hostile questions and confusing prompts. Acknowledge the literal question before reframing; otherwise the audience may experience the answer as evasion.

Related detailed pattern: Answer the decision behind the question

13. State the other side's concern fairly

In simple terms: State the concern fairly → answer it → say what remains uncertain.

Search matches: 149 videos.

Use this in proposals and disagreement. Let the audience recognize their own concern in your version before answering it.

Related detailed pattern: State the strongest objection fairly, then answer it

14. Explain what your experience can—and cannot—show

In simple terms: Explain how your background affects your view → connect it to the point → do not treat it as proof.

Search matches: 141 videos.

Use lived experience to establish perspective and stakes, not automatic authority over every factual question.

Related detailed pattern: Show the role, the mismatch and the conditions for change

15. Separate facts from values

In simple terms: State what happened → name the value judgment → explain the decision it leads to.

Search matches: 123 videos.

Use this in moral, political or organizational presentations. Mark which part is evidence and which part is the value you are asking the audience to share or debate.

Related detailed pattern: Name the standard by which you want the decision judged

16. Define it, give an example, say where it stops

In simple terms: Define the term → give an example → say where the example no longer fits.

Search matches: 117 videos.

Use this for technical or unfamiliar concepts. The example makes the term usable; the boundary prevents it from becoming an empty slogan.

17. Name the risk before asking people to trust you

In simple terms: Name what could go wrong → say how it could be reduced → say who must act.

Search matches: 110 videos.

Use it before a recommendation, commitment or high-stakes request. Audiences usually trust a speaker more when the downside is not hidden.

18. Explain how it may work; do not call that proof

In simple terms: State the point → explain how it might work → say what evidence is still needed.

Use this when explaining why you think a change may lead to a result. Show the steps in between, and say plainly when those steps have not yet been checked.

  • Name the skill or support a hard moment calls for

    Main point: Name what a hard task requires: preparation, coordination, deciding what comes first or asking for help. Explain how someone could obtain that skill or support. Put the pieces together: a full example

The request: approve one small test

This full example is invented for practice. It is not a real archive or proof that the proposed session would work. The speaker coordinates a small community archive. A committee can approve staff time and public events. The speaker asks it to approve one 90-minute session to describe photographs that have been cleared for use.

The invented facts are limited: volunteers scanned 120 photographs, and 45 records are missing a name, place or approximate date. Missing details do not always make a photograph unusable. A visitor's memory may also be uncertain. The speaker cannot promise that the session will identify every photograph, verify its history or grant permission to publish private information.

The first draft

Our digitization project has been a huge success. We have preserved 120 photographs for everyone, but we need to take engagement to the next level. Community memory is our greatest resource, and a workshop will unlock it. We should invite everyone to bring their stories, identify all the missing information and make the archive truly accessible. It will be easy because our volunteers are passionate. Please support this exciting next step.

The draft sounds enthusiastic but leaves the decision unclear. Scanning is treated as if it guaranteed preservation and access. Attendance is treated as if it would identify every photograph. Volunteer enthusiasm is treated as a substitute for staff time. The committee cannot tell what it is approving, what it will cost or what the session is meant to find out. Stronger adjectives would not answer those questions.

The revised presentation

We have scanned 120 photographs. That is real work worth recognizing: volunteers prepared the files, checked the image list and kept the records together. Today I am asking for a separate next step—one ninety-minute session to test whether we can add useful context to a small group of photographs.

Here is the distinction behind the request. Imagine opening a clear scan of a street gathering. You can see the people and the buildings, but the record does not tell you where or when it happened. A scan makes the image available to inspect; it does not supply the missing context. In our current set, 45 records lack at least one name, place or approximate date. That count describes incomplete fields, not 45 images with no value.

The proposed session would use twelve photographs cleared for this purpose. Participants could offer information, say they are uncertain or leave a photograph unidentified. We would record recollections with their uncertainty, not turn every confident answer into a confirmed fact. Questions about verification and permission would remain visible before any new information was published.

The concern about volunteer time is reasonable. I propose two hosts for the ninety-minute session: three volunteer hours between them. Preparation and review would take no more than four additional volunteer hours, making seven hours in total. If people cannot agree to take on that work, we postpone. We would use existing space and equipment, with no new purchases for this test.

We would review three things afterward: how many records gained potentially useful context, which claims still need checking and how much work the session required. Attendance alone will not tell us whether the process helped. We may learn that a different format is needed or that some photographs will remain unidentified.

So the decision today is limited: may we prepare this one session within that time allowance, subject to the material and staffing checks? Approval would not commit us to a recurring program. The scanning milestone remains an achievement. This next step asks what else a reader needs when the image alone cannot answer the question.

A shorter version that keeps the decision clear

This shorter script uses the same invented facts: ‘Our volunteers scanned 120 photographs. Forty-five records still lack a name, place or approximate date. May we prepare one ninety-minute workshop using twelve photographs cleared for this purpose? Two hosts would give three hours between them, with up to four more volunteer hours for preparation and review. We would use existing space and equipment. If we cannot fill those roles, we postpone. Visitors’ suggestions would stay marked as uncertain until checked. Afterward, we would report what information was added, what still needs checking and how much work it took. Today’s request covers one session.’

This version leaves out the imagined street scene and the longer explanation of why scanning matters. It keeps the decision, the relevant numbers, the work required and the conditions for proceeding. To shorten your own talk, first protect the details a listener needs to decide. Then cut examples or explanation that this audience already understands.

Why these choices belong together

The opening credits the volunteers and gives the committee the request at once. It treats 120 scans as completed work, not proof that the archive is ready for every use.

The imagined street scene helps the audience picture why missing details matter. It is clearly made up; it does not claim that a real visitor reacted this way. The number that follows gives a separate fact about the records. The example explains the problem; the count shows its size in this invented case.

The plan is limited: show a cleared set of photographs, collect possible details, mark what is uncertain and review the notes. A meeting cannot turn a memory into verified history. The speaker also answers the concern about staff time by making the proposal smaller and allowing it to be postponed if no one can do the work.

The ending repeats the difference between scanning and adding context, then asks the committee to approve one session. It does not add a new claim at the last moment. Several speaking choices support the same request, but the presentation does not name those techniques.

Prepare for the question that could change the decision

These exchanges continue the invented workshop example. Question: ‘Will this fill the gaps in all 45 records?’ Answer: ‘No. We propose using twelve photographs, and some may remain unidentified. The session would help us find out what information visitors can offer and what work it takes to check it.’ The answer starts with the limit, then explains what the committee could learn from the session.

Question: ‘Who will check whether a visitor’s memory is accurate?’ Answer: ‘We need to assign that work before the session. The four additional hours cover preparation and review together; they may not be enough to settle every question. Any detail we cannot check stays marked as uncertain. I cannot promise that every suggestion will be ready to publish.’ This answer keeps unfinished work visible instead of inventing a person or process that the plan does not yet have.

Question: ‘Why don’t we approve a monthly workshop now?’ Answer: ‘We don’t yet know whether this format gives us useful information at a workload we can sustain. I’m asking for one session. After we review its results and volunteer time, we can bring you a separate proposal for more.’ This keeps the original request clear when someone proposes a larger commitment.

Question: ‘Is the time request four hours or seven?’ Answer: ‘Seven volunteer hours in total: two hosts for ninety minutes each, plus up to four hours shared across preparation and review. We would postpone if that work cannot be assigned.’ State both parts whenever a total could otherwise be mistaken for only one part of the work.

A real version would need to follow the archive's policies, permissions, staffing and fact-checking process. This example is only a speaking exercise, not advice on running an archive or handling legal questions. Replace every invented detail with checked local facts before using a version of it.

Practise the argument, then revise the wording

Read the script aloud and time your own delivery rather than treating a word count as a guaranteed duration. Remove a sentence if it repeats a job already done; retain it if removing it hides a condition the decision needs. Practise a shorter version that keeps the request, the scan/context distinction, the resource limit and the review question.

For a self-check, close the script and write down the exact decision requested, the strongest claim supported by the facts and the main uncertainty. Compare your answers with the script. If another reader is available, ask the same questions without giving them hints. Their response is feedback on this draft, not proof that the approach works for everyone.

Now use your own presentation. Write the decision first, mark every factual claim and identify the evidence it needs. Choose one concrete example, one important limitation and one genuine objection. Draft the ending before polishing the opening. The useful result is a presentation another person can inspect and respond to, not one that merely sounds finished.

Your next draft

Keep the point visible

A useful presentation makes its example, main difference, limits and request clear. Which of those matters most depends on your subject, evidence and audience.

As you revise, ask what may confuse the listener. Add context to an example, a unit to a number, an answer to an objection or a clear request at the end. Fix that before polishing individual phrases.

Remove claims the facts do not support. Keep uncertainty where it matters. Use stories to explain, not to claim more than they show. Ask for a response without assuming the answer will be yes. The goal is to help your audience follow your reasoning and decide what they think.
